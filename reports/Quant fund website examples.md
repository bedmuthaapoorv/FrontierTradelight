# Quant fund sites win by saying less

The clearest finding across every tier of this research — from $100B Renaissance Technologies down to a $5M pre-launch shop — is that credible systematic-fund websites are built on **restraint, not persuasion**: minimal navigation (Home, Strategy/Approach, Team, Research/Insights, Contact, a gated Investor Relations layer, and a Legal/Disclosures footer), near-zero public performance or strategy-mechanics disclosure, and a visual language of dark-or-neutral palettes, generous whitespace, and abstract or human photography rather than stock-chart or trading-floor imagery ([Thomas Digital](https://thomasdigital.com/industry/hedge-fund-websites)). This pattern holds from Renaissance Technologies' almost blank institutional page to Two Sigma's content-rich recruiting hub, and from $1B Swiss CTA Quantica Capital down to a bare-bones $0-marketing-budget shop like Systematic Strategies — the only real outlier is CARL, a retail-facing platform that instead borrows consumer-fintech app UX. For someone building a site for their own proprietary systematic fund that needs to both attract investors and run an investor-relations/reporting portal, the actionable template is: a "thin" public marketing shell that establishes legitimacy through named team credentials, disclosed operational infrastructure, and thought-leadership content, paired with a separate, access-controlled portal for anything resembling performance numbers or offering documents — a split that is as much a legal requirement under Reg D as it is a design choice ([Thomas Digital](https://thomasdigital.com/industry/hedge-fund-websites)).

## Large funds converge on a two-tier site, and size doesn't predict richness

Renaissance Technologies, Two Sigma, AQR, Man Group/Man AHL, D.E. Shaw, Millennium, Winton, Jane Street, Citadel, PDT Partners, and Bridgewater all maintain public sites, but they split into legally cautious "proof of existence" pages (Renaissance, PDT, Winton) versus fuller brand/recruiting sites with research content and culture messaging (Two Sigma, AQR, Man Group, D.E. Shaw, Millennium, Jane Street, Bridgewater) — and this split tracks firm culture and recruiting needs, not AUM: ~$100B Renaissance has one of the thinnest sites on the web, warning visitors that **rentec.com and renfund.com are its only official URLs**, an implicit anti-phishing measure ([rentec.com](https://www.rentec.com/Home.action?index=true)). The recurring page taxonomy across the group is Home → About/Who We Are → What We Do/Approach → Research/Insights → Careers/Join Us → Contact → Investor/Client Login → Legal/Disclosures footer, with firms running SEC-registered public products (AQR's mutual funds via **funds.aqr.com**, Man Group) adding a fund-finder or product-literature section, while pure proprietary-capital firms like Jane Street skip product pages entirely in favor of culture and recruiting content ([aqr.com](https://www.aqr.com/), [janestreet.com](https://www.janestreet.com/)). Design and tone are strikingly convergent: dark navy or near-black backgrounds (Two Sigma, D.E. Shaw) paired with a single accent color (Jane Street's red, Two Sigma's "aurora gradient"), sans-serif type, and copy favoring "research," "rigor," and "curiosity" over sales language — Man Group pairs this restraint with stat call-outs like **"$253.6 billion" AUM and "600+ quants and technologists"** to signal scale without revealing strategy mechanics (fetched homepage content, [man.com](https://www.man.com/)). Citadel is the one firm in this set with a fully modular disclosures architecture — a dedicated `/disclosures/` page broken out by jurisdiction (General, UK Modern Slavery Act, France SFDR/SRD2/Pillar 3) — illustrating how a firm with multiple regulated entities isolates jurisdiction-specific compliance content from brand storytelling ([Citadel Disclosures](https://www.citadel.com/disclosures/)). Across all eleven firms, public disclosure of actual performance numbers or strategy mechanics is essentially zero: what's published is limited to AUM, headcount, founding year, and generic methodology language ("mathematical and statistical methods"), while anything specific sits behind a login at renfund.com, MyAQR, or investor.citadel.com, or is sent only via a confidential offering memorandum — a pattern Bridgewater states explicitly when it tells visitors its offerings are "not available to the general public" (fetched homepage content).

## Boutique and emerging managers substitute compliance rigor for design budget

At the smaller end of the market — Episteme Capital Partners, Systematic Strategies, Drury Capital, Quantica Capital, Crabel Capital, and pre-launch shops like Astant Global Management and MPP&E Capital — the site quality scales visibly with AUM, but the underlying page skeleton stays constant: Home → Strategy/Approach → Team/Firm → (often gated or absent) Track Record → News/Research → Careers → Contact, with an optional password-protected Investor Area appearing only once a fund has an established LP base ([systematic-strategies.com](https://systematic-strategies.com/), [quantica-capital.com](https://quantica-capital.com/)). **Systematic Strategies**, despite "over fifteen years of proven performance" under founder Dr. Jonathan Kinlay, runs a genuinely dated, template-like site with no performance page, no team bios beyond the founder, and no investor login — substituting founder tenure and credentials for visual polish ([systematic-strategies.com](https://systematic-strategies.com/)). **Drury Capital** leads its Investment Products page with risk disclaimers ("significant risk of loss in futures trading... past performance is not indicative of future results") ahead of any return figures, a sequencing choice that itself functions as an institutional-credibility signal ([Drury Investment Products](https://www.drurycapital.com/investment-products/)). By contrast, **Quantica Capital**, now at roughly $1B AUM with a 21-year track record, has scaled its design investment accordingly — illustrative hero artwork, publication cards, an ESG section, and a dedicated Investor Area login — showing that design spend tracks AUM growth rather than being a fixed cost a fund pays upfront ([quantica-capital.com](https://quantica-capital.com/)). **Episteme Capital** requires visitors to click through a legal/regulatory disclaimer before seeing any marketing content at all, a friction pattern common to funds marketing non-public (Reg D) offerings ([Episteme About](https://www.epistemecap.com/about/)). The sole retail-oriented outlier, **CARL** (investwithcarl.com), markets systematic strategies with "15%+ targeted returns" directly to individuals via a mobile-first app, a three-step onboarding funnel, and a gated downloadable performance report reserved for verified accredited investors — proving that consumer-fintech UX is a viable but distinct path chosen only when the target audience is retail rather than institutional ([CARL: How It Works](https://investwithcarl.com/how-carl-works/systematic-trading)). Crucially, across every boutique example, **"Contact Us" is the dominant call-to-action** — not lead-generation forms, pop-ups, or "request a pitch deck" buttons — because these sites function as a credibility layer that supports an already-underway sales conversation (direct outreach, placement agents) rather than a cold-lead funnel ([Drury Investment Products](https://www.drurycapital.com/investment-products/)). A cluster of boutique agencies — Emerson Ward, Typza (Squarespace specialist), Flycast Media, HEDGE Digital — serves exactly this niche, building semi-custom sites on Webflow or Squarespace that bundle brochureware with password-protected investor-document areas, since both platforms offer design polish with low engineering overhead ([Typza](https://www.typza.com/hedge-fund-website-design), [Emerson Ward](https://www.em-ward.com/websites)).

## Reg D exemption choice dictates the entire site architecture

The single biggest architectural fork for a US-based systematic fund is which Reg D exemption it relies on. A **Rule 506(b)** fund cannot use any general solicitation whatsoever — "no website marketing, social media campaigns, mass emails, or public presentations" — and must gate everything behind a pre-existing substantive relationship established before the offering begins; SEC staff has accepted that a password-protected site built on policies designed to establish that relationship first does not itself count as impermissible solicitation ([Procopio](https://www.procopio.com/resource/website-mistake-hurting-private-offering)). A **Rule 506(c)** fund, by contrast, can advertise broadly — "a website, social media, press, conference pitches, and cold outreach to allocators you have never met" — but must take documented "reasonable steps" to verify every purchaser is actually accredited, not merely accept self-certification ([SEC.gov](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/exempt-offerings/general-solicitation-rule-506c)). A March 2025 SEC staff letter gives 506(c) funds a practical shortcut here: a fund with a **minimum investment of at least $200,000 for individuals or $1,000,000 for entities** can generally satisfy verification through written investor representation alone, without additional documentary proof ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/sec-provides-bright-line-test-for-investor-verification-under-rule-506c/)). Layered on top of whichever exemption applies, the SEC's **Investment Advisers Act Marketing Rule (206(4)-1)** governs any "advertisement" regardless of solicitation method: it bans untrue or unsubstantiated claims, requires **net performance to be shown alongside gross performance**, bars performance without specified time periods, forbids cherry-picking ("substantially similar" portfolios must all be shown), and imposes specific disclosure and written-agreement requirements on paid testimonials or endorsements ([SEC.gov Marketing Rule guide](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/small-business-compliance-guides/investment-adviser-marketing)). Records supporting any advertisement — performance data, approvals, testimonial agreements — must be kept for at least five years ([search synthesis, Smarsh](https://www.smarsh.com/regulations/sec-marketing-rule-206-4-1)). The practical upshot, confirmed by the fact that even 506(c) funds in this research still gate detailed track records behind login, is that **the Marketing Rule's stricter substantiation burden makes gating the default choice even when public advertising is legally permitted** — it narrows the "fair and balanced" and recordkeeping obligations to a known, verified audience rather than the general public. The UK's FCA achieves a functionally similar result through financial-promotion rules restricting high-risk investment marketing to certified high-net-worth, sophisticated, or restricted investor categories, approved or communicated only by an FCA-authorised firm ([FCA PS22/10](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps22-10.pdf)). On top of exemption-specific rules, nearly every large fund researched (Renaissance, AQR, D.E. Shaw) now runs a prominent **anti-impersonation/fraud warning** naming its only official domains — a defensive pattern plausibly driven by a wave of social-media investment scams impersonating known fund brands, and one worth adopting even for a small fund with any public profile (fetched homepage content).

## The investor portal itself can be bought, borrowed, or built minimally

For the reporting-portal half of the brief, the market splits into three tiers. **Fund-admin-bundled SaaS** platforms — Juniper Square, Allvue Systems (90,000+ LP users), Carta, AngelList, FundCount — let the GP own a branded portal combining performance/NAV data, a document vault (K-1s, subscription docs, audited financials), capital call and distribution notices, e-signature workflows, and self-service wire-instruction updates, typically with enterprise-only pricing (FundCount starts at **$34,899/year** plus hosting fees) ([Juniper Square](https://www.junipersquare.com/platform/portal), [FundCount](https://fundcount.com/best-investor-portal-software-private-market-fund-managers/)). **Administrator-hosted shared portals** — Citco's CitcoOne, Northern Trust's InvestCloud-built portal, SS&C's single-portal-across-managers model — instead brand the login to the administrator rather than the fund; established manager Chilton Investment Company, for instance, simply routes investors from its own "My Account" page to Citco's **citcoone.citco.com**, trading UI control for zero build cost ([Chilton My Account](https://chiltonfunds.com/my-account)). **Generic white-label tools** like Clinked provide a document vault, granular LP-level permissions, audit logging, and secure messaging without any underlying fund-accounting engine, serving as a lightweight stand-in when NAV and statements are already computed elsewhere ([Clinked](https://www.clinked.com/blog/best-investor-portal-software)). For funds below the threshold where any of this is affordable, "fund-in-a-box" emerging-manager packages like Repool bundle a basic portal into a flat annual fee (**$20,000/year plus a $10,000 one-time fee** for its Emerging Fund Plan), while a documented DIY pattern for $25–75M funds uses Airtable or a cloud database as the data warehouse, Python or R for calculations, and Power BI or Tableau for dashboards, delivered via automated email or a lightweight gated page ([Repool](https://repool.com/launch/hedge-fund/), [Finantrix buyer's guide](https://www.finantrix.com/buyer-guides/hedge-fund-operations-platforms-emerging-managers)). Whichever tier is chosen, the baseline feature set worth replicating is narrow: MFA-gated login, a document vault organized by type and date, a per-investor performance/capital-account view (not just fund-level NAV), and a simple capital-call/distribution notice mechanism — e-signature, engagement analytics, and CRM integration are enterprise extras unlikely to be worth building for a one- or few-person fund ([Juniper Square](https://www.junipersquare.com/platform/portal)). On the UX-gating question specifically, the standard approach layers two distinct checkpoints: a lightweight, non-binding accredited-investor self-certification or login wall in front of the Investor Relations section (limiting casual public access and general-solicitation exposure), and a separate, more rigorous verification step — documents, a third-party service like VerifyInvestor.com, or the $200k/$1M bright-line representation — only at actual subscription time ([VerifyInvestor](https://www.verifyinvestor.com/), [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/sec-provides-bright-line-test-for-investor-verification-under-rule-506c/)). No example of a quant/systematic fund's own investor-portal URL structure was found in this research; the clearest sourced patterns (Juniper Square customers, Virtus, Chilton/Citco) come from real estate, mutual-fund, and administrator-routed hedge-fund contexts respectively, so a quant fund building its own would be charting slightly novel territory in branding terms, even though the underlying feature set is well established.

## Conclusion

The synthesis across all four research threads points to one coherent build plan: a thin public site (Home, Strategy, Team, Research/Insights, Contact, Legal footer with impersonation and performance disclaimers) built on a restrained visual system — dark-or-neutral base, one accent color, no stock-chart or trading-floor imagery — paired with a visibly linked "Investor Login" leading to either a purchased portal (Repool-style bundle or a Clinked-style document vault) or a minimal homegrown page showing per-investor NAV, statements, and documents behind MFA. The decision that cascades through everything else is the Reg D exemption: choosing 506(b) means the public site can carry no solicitation content at all and the "attract investors" job falls entirely to offline relationship-building, while 506(c) permits a genuinely public-facing marketing site but obligates rigorous, documented accredited-investor verification and full Marketing Rule compliance (net-vs-gross performance, no cherry-picking, five-year recordkeeping) on anything that reads as an advertisement — a reason most funds researched, even those legally free to advertise, still choose to gate their real track record behind a login rather than publish it.
