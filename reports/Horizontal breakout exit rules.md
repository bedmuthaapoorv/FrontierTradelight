# Layered exits beat any single stop rule

The best-supported exit architecture for a mechanical horizontal-breakout system is not one rule but four layered ones, sequenced by trade age and progress: a fast structural failure check in the first few days, a fixed-percentage hard stop as an absolute backstop, an ATR-based trailing stop that takes over once the trade is working, and O'Neil-style profit-taking/topping logic that governs when to sell strength rather than weakness. Every component in this stack is computable from daily OHLCV alone. The evidence base is strongest — large samples, primary sources — for the failure-detection and profit-taking percentages (O'Neil's 7-8% stop and 20-25% profit-take, backed by a 197,000-trade O'Neil Global Advisors study, and Bulkowski's multi-thousand-pattern failure-rate statistics), and weakest for the exact ATR multiplier to use, where the literature is directional-consensus-but-not-rigorously-backtested. A 26-year, 105,708-trade study on Russell 1000 stocks provides the strongest single piece of evidence that removing fixed profit targets in favor of trend-following exits (calendar-based or trailing-channel) outperforms fixed-target "profit-chasing," directly supporting a let-winners-run design for the 1-90 day holding window this system targets.

## Fast failure detection hinges on close-based re-entry, not intraday wicks

The literature converges cleanly on how to distinguish a genuine failed breakout from a normal retest. Bulkowski's pattern statistics — drawn from 900 to over 10,000 "perfect trades" across rectangle tops/bottoms and 23 pattern types spanning 1991-2008 — show that **throwbacks and pullbacks to the breakout level occur in 53-66% of all breakouts** and are explicitly *not* failures; they typically resolve within **3 to 10 days** ([Bulkowski Rectangle Bottoms](https://thepatternsite.com/rectbots.html), [Bulkowski on Pullbacks](https://www.thepatternsite.com/pullbacks.html)). A true failure, by contrast, is defined structurally as price **closing back inside the prior base/range**, not merely touching or wicking below the pivot intraday ([LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/5-false-breakout-strategies-for-traders/)). This gives a directly implementable rule: require a *daily close* below the pivot (ideally back inside the base) before flagging failure, never an intraday low. A small but concrete backtest (n=77 opening-range-breakout fills) found this distinction has real discriminating power even at a same-day horizon — closes above the entry price averaged **+0.15R with a 55% win rate**, while closes below averaged **-0.16R with a 41% win rate** ([Qullamaggie Breakout Scanner discussion](https://github.com/VladPetrariu/Qullamaggie-breakout-scanner/issues/7)). Base-rate failure statistics reinforce urgency: Bulkowski's aggregate study found failure-to-reach-10% rates of **14% in the 1990s bull market rising to 28% in 2003-2007** for upward breakouts, with volume confirmation cutting failure rates roughly in half ([Bulkowski Failure Rate Study](https://thepatternsite.com/FailureRates.html)). The practical synthesis for a mechanical system: check for a close back inside the base or below the pivot within the first 3-10 trading days (matching the throwback-resolution window), and treat this as a distinct, tighter exit tier from the general hard stop described next — O'Neil's own 15-rule framework lists "sell on failed breakouts" as a category separate from the 7-8% stop, though no primary source gives it its own distinct percentage, leaving this as a design choice rather than an externally validated constant.

## O'Neil's 7-8% stop and 20-25% profit rule form the backbone, validated at scale

The single best-evidenced pair of exit numbers in this entire research base is O'Neil's paired discipline: **cut losses at 7-8% below the actual entry fill price, take most profits at 20-25% gain from the buy point** ([ChartMill](https://www.chartmill.com/documentation/trading-and-investing/methodologies/527-William-ONeils-7-8-Sell-Rule-Explained), [IBD via X](https://x.com/IBDinvestors/status/1755350824581034256)). The rationale is explicitly convexity-based — the loss-recovery math worsens nonlinearly past 7-8%, and O'Neil's own historical review of past market leaders found "most healthy stocks do not fall more than 7-8% below a proper buy point." Critically, this is measured from the entry fill, not the pivot, intraday high, or a moving average, and it is framed as a *ceiling*, not a target — tighter position-specific stops (e.g., below the pivot) are expected to fire first for most trades. The clearest quantitative validation of this whole framework comes from a primary O'Neil Global Advisors study, "Breakouts: Pump Up the Volume" (Marble, Ognar, Mirzakhanian, May 2022), which measured **63-trading-day forward returns on ~197,000 breakout events** (1995-2021, U.S. stocks) segmented by breakout-day volume surge. Hit rates clustered near **57% across all volume buckets**, with average gains of 12.9-19.9% and average losses of 11.4-14.5% as volume-surge magnitude rose from <50% to ≥150% above the 50-day average — and outperformance rose monotonically with volume, with the ≥150%-volume bucket generating **5.04% cumulative return and 2.79% alpha** versus 2.61%/0.67% for the lowest-volume bucket ([O'Neil Global Advisors PDF](https://www.oneilglobaladvisors.com/documents/FG/oneil/research/617948_OGA_Breakouts-OGA.pdf)). Because this study applied no stop-loss at all, its raw average losses (11-14.5%) are naturally wider than O'Neil's 7-8% discretionary rule — implying a mechanical 7-8% stop would have exited a meaningful share of that study's losing trades earlier, for better realized risk than the unstopped numbers suggest. The profit side carries an important override: if a stock **gains 20%+ within the first three weeks of breaking out**, O'Neil's system directs holding for a minimum of eight weeks rather than selling into strength, because early explosive gains historically flag future market leaders ([IBD via Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/know-invoke-8-week-hold-215800238.html)). This 20%-in-15-trading-days / 40-trading-day-minimum-hold branch is fully mechanical from OHLCV date indices and fits naturally inside the 1-90 day holding window specified for this system.

## Volatility-scaled trailing stops should run 2.5x-3.5x ATR once a trade is working

For winners that clear the initial failure and stop-loss checks, ATR-based trailing stops are the best-documented mechanism for capturing open-ended gains, but the exact multiplier is a genuine area of soft consensus rather than rigorous science. The **Chandelier Exit** — Highest High minus (ATR × multiplier), using a 22-day lookback — has a canonical default multiplier of **3.0**, with "2 to 3" cited as the common range across independent sources ([StockCharts ChartSchool](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-overlays/chandelier-exit)). Wilder's original 1978 Volatility System anchors instead to the "significant close" (most favorable close since entry) rather than the extreme high, reportedly using a **multiplier around 2.8-3.1 on a 7-day ATR**, though this figure could only be confirmed via secondary paraphrase, not the primary text ([LizardIndicators](https://www.lizardindicators.com/volatility-stop/)). The Turtle Trading system, arguably the most battle-tested precedent, used a **2N (roughly 2× a 20-day ATR) hard stop** combined with a *separate, much tighter* trailing exit (10-day low for the faster system, 20-day low for the slower one) — meaning the Turtles' effective in-trade risk tightened considerably below the initial catastrophic stop once a position was profitable ([TrendSpider](https://trendspider.com/learning-center/richard-dennis-turtle-trading-strategy/)). That system delivered roughly **80% average annual returns across the 1983-1988 experiment**, though these figures come from popularized retellings rather than an audited record and should be treated as approximate. On multiplier selection specifically, style-segmented guidance converges: **day traders around 1.5-2x ATR, swing traders 2-3x, position traders/trend-followers 3-4x** ([QuantVPS](https://www.quantvps.com/blog/atr-stop-loss)) — given this system's 1-90 day horizon spans the swing-to-position boundary, a multiplier in the **2.5x-3.5x band** is the best-supported choice, and it is fully computable from daily H/L/C with no intraday dependency. One quantitative backtest (200 S&P 500 swing setups, 2020-2024) claimed a 22/3x Chandelier Exit reduced premature stop-outs by 34% versus fixed-dollar stops with a 51.3% win rate and -10.2% max drawdown, but this source discloses no methodology or data provenance and should be treated as illustrative rather than validated ([Volatility Box](https://volatilitybox.com/research/volatility-adjusted-stop-losses/) — flagged low-confidence). No rigorous, reproducible sensitivity study comparing 2x vs. 2.5x vs. 3x vs. 4x on a standardized universe exists in the available literature; the ATR-period choice (14 vs. 20 vs. 21) similarly rests on heuristic "match period to expected hold length" guidance rather than backtested sensitivity data ([search synthesis](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/average-true-range-dynamic-stop-loss-levels/)).

## Letting winners run beats fixed targets, but topping signals still matter for exhausted trends

The strongest single piece of evidence favoring a let-winners-run design over any fixed take-profit target comes from a 26-year, 105,708-trade backtest on Russell 1000 stocks (200-day MA crossover entries, 56 exit rules compared): a fixed-target-style "Profit-Chaser" exit was the **worst-performing rule tested**, averaging -0.02% to -0.14% per trade, while a pure calendar-based (time-held) exit averaged **+0.52% to +1.03% per trade**, and a Donchian-channel trailing-low exit scored +0.24% — the author's conclusion being "the exit that wins most often makes the least money" ([Setup4Alpha](https://setup4alpha.substack.com/p/trailing-stop-vs-fixed-stop-tested)). This directly supports pairing a horizontal-breakout entry with a trailing-stop-dominant exit rather than a hard profit cap, consistent with the Turtle system's own no-target philosophy. That said, this effect appears trend-following- and equity-specific: a separate crypto-focused backtest found trailing stops underperformed a fixed -3.5% stop by about 8% in total profit, a reminder that regime and asset class matter ([TradeZella](https://www.tradezella.com/blog/stop-loss-strategies) — lower-confidence, unverified methodology). Within the "let it run" camp, O'Neil's technical topping toolkit supplies the mechanism for knowing *when* a winning trend is exhausted rather than merely trailing blindly: a close below the 50-day SMA on above-average volume, "stalling" days (heavy volume, weak close, little price progress), climax runs (25-50% price gains over 2-3 weeks occurring roughly 18+ weeks after the original breakout, often ending in an exhaustion gap), and market-wide distribution-day counts are all cited as O'Neil sell triggers used in combination rather than alone ([X/MARKET INSIGHTS! thread](https://x.com/IManghaila/status/2066477677947543634), [Yahoo Finance on climax tops](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/vertical-logic-learning-read-sell-215900159.html)). All of these except the market-wide distribution-day count (which requires index data, a permitted input here per the sector-rank/breadth allowance) are computable from single-stock OHLCV. Complementary exhaustion signals from general technical-analysis sources — RSI persisting above 80-90 during a parabolic run, combined with a high-volume day that fails to make a new high — corroborate the same idea but carry an explicit caveat that RSI alone is unreliable as a standalone trigger during genuine parabolic trends, since overbought readings can persist for extended periods without reversing ([TradeFundrr](https://tradefundrr.com/parabolic-move-exhaustion-setups/), [fxopen](https://fxopen.com/blog/en/what-is-a-parabolic-arc-pattern-and-how-can-you-trade-it/)). On mechanical position-scaling, the R-multiple framework (close 50% at +1R, another 25% at +2R, trail the remainder with an ATR stop, moving the stop to breakeven after the first scale-out) recurs across independent sources as the dominant practitioner convention, and is directly compatible with O'Neil's 20-25%/8-week-hold branch as an alternative fixed-percentage scaling scheme ([LuxAlgo Library](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/scaling-out/), [EdgeFlo](https://www.edgeflo.com/blog/partial-profits-trading)).

## Conclusion

Synthesizing across all four research threads, the best-supported design is a sequenced state machine rather than a static stop: days 0-10 use a tight structural failure check (close back below pivot/inside the base) layered under the 7-8%-from-entry hard stop; once a position is confirmed working, control passes to a 2.5x-3.5x ATR trailing stop (Chandelier-style, anchored to the post-entry highest high) that lets the trade run with no fixed price ceiling, per the strongest available large-sample evidence against fixed profit targets; the 20-25% O'Neil profit-taking checkpoint acts as a partial scale-out trigger rather than a mandatory full exit, suppressed by the 20%-in-3-weeks override that extends the minimum hold to 8 weeks for a stock showing unusually strong early momentum; and O'Neil's topping cluster (50-day MA break on volume, stalling days, climax-run detection, and — if index data is used — distribution-day counts) provides the late-stage aggressive-sell trigger for trades that have already run substantially. The biggest remaining uncertainty is not which rules to include but how to weight and sequence them precisely under backtesting, since no source in this research base tested the O'Neil rule set, the ATR multiplier choice, or the interaction between structural failure exits and volatility trailing stops as an integrated system — every quantitative study found examined these components in isolation (raw forward returns, exit-rule comparisons, or pattern failure statistics), never as the combined pipeline a production system actually needs, meaning in-house backtesting on this specific universe and horizon remains a required next step rather than an optional refinement.
