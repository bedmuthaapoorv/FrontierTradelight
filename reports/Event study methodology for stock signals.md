# Measuring and timing contract-news trading edges

Academic event-study methodology gives a clear, well-validated recipe for judging a contract-announcement signal like a naval or defense contract-award reaction: measure abnormal returns against a market-model benchmark fit over a 100+ day estimation window, aggregate them over a short (roughly 1-to-5 day) event window, and test significance with a variance-corrected statistic such as **Boehmer-Musumeci-Poulsen (BMP)** rather than a plain t-test — while the optimal holding period is not a single universal number but a magnitude-conditional one: the two most relevant US studies of government/defense contract awards find the **pooled average reaction is economically negligible and statistically insignificant** (3-to-5 basis points), and that a real, tradeable, multi-day drift only appears in specific subsets — contracts large relative to the firm's market cap, Army-awarded contracts, short-duration contracts, and contract *modifications* — where 5-day returns are larger and more significant than 3-day returns. This means the "optimal exit" question is really two separable questions — how big is the news relative to the firm, and does that subset historically show continued drift — and volatility-scaled exit theory (ATR-based stops, options-market expected-move math, CTA/momentum practice) supplies a principled way to convert a stock's own volatility into a holding-period and stop-distance once the announcement's subset has been identified, rather than picking a fixed day-count for every announcement. Critically, no rigorous academic study of Indian defense-PSU or Ministry of Defence/PIB-driven announcements exists at all, so every magnitude and timing figure below is a US-market extrapolation carrying real, stated uncertainty, and the report treats that uncertainty as a first-class finding rather than a footnote.

## The reaction should be measured as an abnormal return against a fitted benchmark, not a raw price move

The methodologically correct way to judge whether a contract announcement "worked" is never to look at the raw stock return on the day — it is to compute an **abnormal return**: actual return minus a model's predicted "normal" return, estimated from a clean pre-event window. This procedure, first codified by Fama, Fisher, Jensen & Roll in 1969 and systematized by MacKinlay's 1997 survey, fits a **market model** (the firm's return regressed on a market index over an estimation window) using **100–250 trading days ending before the event window**, and results "are not sensitive to varying estimation-window lengths as long as the window exceeds about 100 days" ([EventStudyTools Application Blueprint](https://www.eventstudytools.com/event-study-application-blueprint)). Abnormal returns on individual days aggregate into the standard trio of statistics: **AAR** (average abnormal return across firms on a given day), **CAR** (cumulative abnormal return across days for one firm), and **CAAR** (cumulative average across both firms and days) ([EventStudyTools](https://www.eventstudytools.com/event-study-application-blueprint)). For a strategy judging a small number of announcements rather than pooling hundreds of firm-events, the choice between the market model and the simpler market-adjusted model (alpha=0, beta=1) matters far less than getting the estimation window right and screening it for confounding news — the standard six-step workflow explicitly calls for checking "both the estimation and event windows for unrelated material news that could bias parameter estimates or contaminate the isolated event effect" ([EventStudyTools](https://www.eventstudytools.com/event-study-application-blueprint)). Long-horizon buy-and-hold abnormal returns (BHAR) against a matched benchmark are the correct tool only if the strategy also wants to measure drift over 6–12 months; Kothari & Warner's 2007 review concludes "short-horizon methods are quite reliable, while long-horizon methods have improved but serious limitations remain" ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=608601)), and Lyon, Barber & Tsai (1999) show BHARs are positively skewed in ways that bias standard long-horizon t-statistics ([Stern NYU PDF](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~eofek/PhD/papers/LBT_Improved_JF.pdf)) — a real cost that a short-reaction defense-contract signal can mostly sidestep.

Event-window length is a genuine bias/noise tradeoff, not a solved parameter: too short a window misses information leakage, too long a window risks contaminating the measurement with unrelated news ([MASEconomics synthesis](https://maseconomics.com/event-study-methodology-explained/)). The literature-wide convention for discrete, scheduled announcements is a symmetric window of **1 to 11 trading days, most commonly 5 days** ([EventStudyTools](https://www.eventstudytools.com/event-study-application-blueprint)), and because contract awards are public, scheduled-release announcements rather than slowly-diffusing rumors, the leakage risk that would justify a longer window is comparatively low. Krivin, Patton, Rose & Tabak (2003) specifically address the single-security case this project resembles, showing that windows extended until abnormal returns stop accumulating — rather than a fixed arbitrary length — correlate meaningfully with the size of the underlying news shock ([NERA PDF](https://www.nera.com/content/dam/nera/publications/archive1/6394.pdf)), which is directly usable as a per-announcement validation check layered on top of a standard fixed baseline window.

## Significance testing must correct for event-induced volatility and same-day clustering, or it will overstate the edge

A plain t-test on abnormal returns is the wrong default for this use case. The **Patell (1976)** standardized-residual Z-test improves on it but "over-rejects when announcement-day variance spikes" because it assumes the event itself doesn't raise volatility ([EventStudyTools: Significance Tests](https://www.eventstudytools.com/significance-tests)) — an assumption almost certainly violated by contract-award announcements, which plausibly do raise announcement-day volatility. The **Boehmer-Musumeci-Poulsen (1991) standardized cross-sectional t-test ("BMP")** fixes this by additionally scaling by the event-day cross-sectional standard deviation, so "the denominator widens automatically when the event makes everyone volatile," and it is now described as "the standard parametric default for short-window event studies" ([EventStudyTools](https://www.eventstudytools.com/significance-tests)) — making it the right default test here, run alongside a nonparametric check (rank or generalized sign test) as a robustness cross-check, since practitioner guidance recommends running "one parametric and one non-parametric test that target the threats your sample actually faces" and treating agreement as the real evidence ([EventStudyTools](https://www.eventstudytools.com/significance-tests)). A second correction that is easy to miss but directly relevant to a defense/procurement signal is **event clustering**: when several small-cap contractors announce awards from the same government budget cycle on the same calendar day, their abnormal returns are correlated, which "inflates the apparent sample size and understates the standard errors unless the affected firms are treated as one portfolio" ([bookdown.org synthesis](https://bookdown.org/mike/data_analysis/sec-event-studies.html)); the **Kolari-Pynnonen (2010)** adjustment factor, √[(1−r̄)/(1+(N−1)r̄)], corrects for this, and even a small average pairwise correlation of r̄=0.02 across N=100 firms inflates the true standard error to roughly **1.4x** the naive estimate ([EventStudyTools](https://www.eventstudytools.com/significance-tests)). On sample size, the concentrated, single-day nature of a contract-reaction (as opposed to a diffuse multi-month effect) is favorable for statistical power: a 10% abnormal effect concentrated in one day can be detected with 100% power from a sample of just **6 stocks**, whereas the same cumulative effect spread over six months needs a sample of **200** to be detected even 65% of the time ([ResearchGate discussion](https://www.researchgate.net/post/Statistical_Power_for_an_Event_Study)) — meaning a modest historical sample of dozens (not hundreds) of contract announcements can plausibly support meaningful inference on a short window, provided the test statistic is chosen correctly and thin-trading noise (a real risk for small-cap defense names, which can exhibit systematic upward bias in multi-day cumulative abnormal returns from bid-ask bounce) is screened for.

## The pooled average reaction to a defense contract award is small and often statistically indistinguishable from zero

This is the single most important, and most counter-intuitive, finding for judging a contract-award signal: the broad, unconditional average abnormal return to winning a government or defense contract is **economically negligible**. Elayan, Pukthuanthong & Roll's UCLA Anderson study of 1,963 corporate-government contract announcements (1990–2000) finds a statistically significant but small average abnormal return for government contractors of **0.54%** concentrated in the two-day window from the day before to the day of announcement, versus a larger **1.13%–1.43%** for purely inter-corporate (non-government) contracts — government contract winners gain systematically *less* than winners of private-sector contracts ([UCLA Anderson working paper](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/20-05.pdf)). Returns in the days following the announcement (t=+1 to +5) were statistically indistinguishable from zero in that study, and the contract-*granting* party showed no significant reaction at all, suggesting the market resolves this information almost entirely within the announcement window with no discernible drift in that sample ([UCLA Anderson](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/20-05.pdf)). A far larger and more recent (2026) proprietary dataset from TenderAlpha and the University of Hong Kong, covering 16,529 unique contract announcements across roughly 183 US-listed defense equities from 2010–2025, confirms and sharpens this picture at scale: the pooled **3-day value-weighted return is +0.6 basis points (t=0.18, not significant)** and the **5-day equal-weighted return is +7.5 basis points (t=1.75)** — the average, unconditional defense contract announcement carries essentially no reliably tradeable signal ([TenderAlpha/HKU white paper](https://www.tenderalpha.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/White-Paper-Defense-Contract-Announcements-TenderAlpha-HKU-1.pdf)).

The tradeable signal, where it exists, lives entirely in specific conditional subsets, and this is the key practical takeaway for judging any individual announcement. Contracts in the **top 5% by size relative to the firm's market cap** show a highly significant 3-day value-weighted return of **+30 basis points (t=2.35)**; contracts large relative to total assets show a similarly significant **+28.1 basis points (t=2.24)** — while absolute dollar contract size alone shows only a weak, mostly insignificant pattern, meaning **economic materiality relative to firm size, not headline contract value, is what should gate whether a signal is expected to have any edge at all** ([TenderAlpha/HKU](https://www.tenderalpha.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/White-Paper-Defense-Contract-Announcements-TenderAlpha-HKU-1.pdf)). Since Indian shipbuilding and defense-PSU announcements are frequently large relative to the (often mid-cap) contractor's market capitalization, this specific subset finding is the most directly transferable piece of US evidence to the Indian use case, even though it comes from a different market entirely. A separate small pharmaceutical-sector study (327 event-days, 12 NASDAQ firms, 2010–2021) reinforces the caution against expecting a uniform sharp jump: it found statistically significant *cumulative* abnormal returns over a 20-day window but insignificant *individual daily* abnormal returns, concluding "winning a government contract has a limited impact on the stock market" in that sector and hinting the effect can show up gradually rather than as a single spike ([pharma contract-award study](https://www.anserpress.org/journal/jea/3/1/43)).

## Multi-day drift is real but concentrated in specific subsets — modifications, Army awards, and short-duration contracts

The dominant pattern across the literature is fast, largely efficient pricing within one to two days for a *generic* announcement, with Elayan et al. finding the largest average abnormal return actually falls on the day *before* the public announcement (t=-1), attributed to information leakage or reporting lag rather than genuine post-announcement underreaction ([UCLA Anderson](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/20-05.pdf)). But the TenderAlpha/HKU paper finds statistically significant continued drift beyond day one in three specific, identifiable subsets: for **short-duration (≤1 year) contracts**, the 5-day equal-weighted return (+28.6 bps, t=2.84) is nearly triple the 3-day return (+11.5 bps, t=1.44); for **contract modifications** (changes to existing contracts, as opposed to brand-new awards), the 5-day return (+12.8 bps, t=2.07) is both larger and more significant than the 3-day return (+8.2 bps, t=1.68); and Army-awarded contracts show a similar pattern ([TenderAlpha/HKU](https://www.tenderalpha.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/White-Paper-Defense-Contract-Announcements-TenderAlpha-HKU-1.pdf)). Notably, the paper's explicit conclusion that "modifications show stronger post-announcement drift than new awards" runs counter to the naive assumption that the *initial* award is the most information-rich event — a finding reinforced by Elayan et al., who separately tested whether being a firm's first ("initial") contract win in their sample predicted a bigger reaction and found the effect statistically **insignificant**, describing the null result as "somewhat unexpected" ([UCLA Anderson](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/20-05.pdf)). Neither study, however, examined physical execution-stage milestones specifically (keel-laying, steel-cutting, commissioning, first delivery) — no dedicated academic study of shipbuilding lifecycle milestones as a distinct event-study category was located in this research, which is a genuine evidence gap rather than a search failure, so any expectation about how a naval-milestone announcement compares to an initial award announcement is an inference by analogy to "modifications," not a directly confirmed finding.

Anchoring the multi-day drift question in the broader academic literature on post-announcement drift adds useful outer bounds. Classic post-earnings-announcement drift (PEAD, Ball & Brown 1968; Bernard & Thomas 1989) documents roughly **2% drift over 60 trading days** following an earnings surprise, with the largest share of that drift — about 20 trading days' worth — concentrated in the first month and a long tail extending to 60–90 days ([Bernard & Thomas 1989](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/joares/v27y1989ip1-36.html); [PEAD review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214635020303750)). A directly analogous study of military contract awards around 9/11 explicitly adopted this same 60-trading-day long-run convention, finding no significant short-run difference in announcement-day reactions before versus after 9/11, but that post-9/11 contracts appeared "underfunded" by the 60-day long-run measure ([Palkar, Larson & Larson 2012](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09603107.2011.607126)) — suggesting that if a contract-award signal wants to also test for a slower, quarter-scale drift (distinct from the days-to-a-week reaction), the 60-trading-day PEAD convention is the best-evidenced horizon to borrow, not an arbitrary number. At the far end, time-series momentum research (Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen 2012, across 58 futures markets 1985–2009) documents trend persistence of roughly 12 months before partial reversal over the following 1–2 years ([NYU Stern preprint](https://w4.stern.nyu.edu/facdir/lpederse/papers/TimeSeriesMomentum.pdf); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X11002613)) — a useful sanity-check upper bound (roughly a year) on how long any documented drift phenomenon has been shown to run before decaying, well beyond what any contract-announcement study suggests is realistic for this signal type.

## Volatility-scaled exit rules give a principled, instrument-agnostic way to size the holding period and stop, once the subset is known

Once a given announcement is classified into a subset expected to carry real drift (large relative-size, modification, short-duration, Army-type), the question shifts from "is there an effect" to "how do I size the exit." Here, the volatility-scaled exit literature — distinct from event-study methodology proper — gives tools that generalize across instruments rather than being fit to one dataset. **ATR-based stops** are the standard practitioner mechanism: J. Welles Wilder's 1978 Average True Range normalizes stop distance to a stock's own recent "noise level," so a stop several ATRs away is unlikely to be triggered by ordinary chop rather than a genuine reversal ([StockCharts ChartSchool](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-indicators/average-true-range-atr)). The Turtle Trading system (Richard Dennis & William Eckhardt) sets initial stops at **2N** (2x a 20-day ATR) and sizes positions so a 1N move equals 1% of equity, capping initial risk at 2% per position ([Turtle Trading Strategy](https://www.quantifiedstrategies.com/turtle-trading-strategy/)), while Le Beau's **Chandelier Exit** uses a wider **3x ATR(22)** trail from the rolling high/low specifically to let a working trend "breathe" through normal pullbacks ([StockCharts Chandelier Exit](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-overlays/chandelier-exit)) — the literature is explicit that none of these specific multiples (2N, 2x, 2.8–3.1x, 3x) are derived from a closed-form statistical optimization; they are practitioner heuristics validated by long-run track records, not proofs. Importantly, the momentum/CTA literature and the Turtle system converge on expressing exits in **volatility-normalized units** (ATR multiples, percent excess return) rather than fixed calendar days or fixed price amounts specifically *because* that is what lets the same rule transfer across instruments as different as bond futures and commodities without re-fitting ([Trend following, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trend_following_trading)) — the same design principle applies directly to generalizing a contract-announcement exit rule across defense-sector names of very different volatility.

The options-pricing literature supplies a genuinely closed-form bridge from volatility to a holding-period estimate. Because returns are commonly modeled as a random walk, standard deviation of price (the "expected move") scales with the **square root of time**, not linearly: Expected Move = Price × IV × √(T/365) ([expected-move formula, multiple sources](https://volatilitybox.com/research/expected-move-options/)). Inverting this relationship — given a stock's ATR as a proxy for daily σ and a target move of k×ATR — yields an expected number of trading days T ≈ (target move / daily σ)² to reach that target under a no-drift random-walk assumption, giving a volatility-derived time-based exit rather than an arbitrary fixed day-count; this specific inversion is a reasonable mathematical inference from the cited literature rather than a named, established technique in its own right, and the random-walk assumption underlying it is known to be violated by exactly the jump and volatility-clustering dynamics that news-driven announcements produce. Formal optimal-stopping theory is more cautionary than prescriptive here: under a pure geometric Brownian motion with constant positive drift, it can be optimal to *never* use a trailing stop at all, and once more realistic features (mean-reversion, time-varying or decaying drift, combined stop-and-limit orders) are introduced, the literature requires numerical solutions rather than a clean closed-form multiple ([arXiv:1701.03960, "Optimal Trading with a Trailing Stop"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.03960)). One useful, counter-intuitive result from this branch: as volatility rises, the theoretically optimal *sell* trigger moves further away, not closer — a rational stopping rule tolerates more noise from a more volatile instrument rather than tightening against it, which is consistent with (though for a different underlying reason than) the ATR-scaling convention of using a wider stop in points for higher-volatility names.

## Conclusion

The methodologically defensible way to judge a naval/defense contract-announcement signal is a two-stage procedure, not a single backtest statistic: first, classify the announcement by its economic materiality relative to the firm (large-relative-size, modification vs. new award, contract duration, awarding branch) using a BMP-style, cluster-corrected event study over a standard short window, because the pooled unconditional average reaction across all such announcements is small and often statistically indistinguishable from zero — treating every contract announcement as equally "tradeable" is not supported by the evidence. Second, for announcements that fall into a subset with documented drift, size the holding period and stop using volatility-normalized rules (ATR multiples, the square-root-of-time expected-move relationship) rather than a fixed day-count, since that is the design principle that lets an exit rule generalize across differently-volatile names instead of being fit to one instrument or one historical sample. The most consequential open uncertainty is geographic: essentially the entire evidentiary base here is American, and no rigorous academic study of Indian defense-PSU reactions to Ministry of Defence or PIB announcements exists — real-time market commentary confirms Indian defense stocks do move multiple percent on MoD/DAC news, but with no formal significance testing behind those figures — so every number in this report should be read as a transferable prior to be validated against India-specific data, not a plug-in parameter, and the biggest research payoff from here would be a dedicated, properly-specified event study of Indian PIB-driven announcements rather than further refinement of US-market methodology.
