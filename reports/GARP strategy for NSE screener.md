# Build GARP as a slow, composite sleeve

GARP for the Nifty 500 should not be a single "PEG < 1" rule bolted onto 5–40 day swing trades. It works best as a **separate, quarterly-refreshed portfolio sleeve** that ranks stocks first on multi-year growth (3-year EPS and sales CAGR), then on a quality-plus-value composite (ROE, leverage, P/E relative to sector), with hard gates for positive earnings history, a growth ceiling, liquidity and separate treatment of financials. This is the S&P DJI GARP recipe, rebuilt with NSE's own factor building blocks. The evidence for a plain PEG screen is weak: the best public US test found that GARP's edge depends on the start date and largely disappears once loss-making stocks are excluded. India's documented premia are in the components (quality, momentum, value and earnings revisions), not in the PEG ratio itself. Backtesting is limited mainly by data, not code. No affordable, documented point-in-time (as-first-reported) fundamentals source exists for NSE, and no affordable historical analyst-consensus source exists either. So the backtest should run on trailing statement data, lagged to SEBI filing deadlines, over a point-in-time Nifty 500 membership list. Forward-estimate signals (forward PEG, revisions) should go into the live screener only. Building this in FrontierTradeLight needs four new capabilities: a fundamentals pipeline with filing-date stamps, a historical index-constituents table, a portfolio rebalancer with rank buffers and caps, and a results-calendar feed. A GARP-filtered version of the existing technical system is a reasonable second build, but GARP will act there as a guardrail, not as the source of alpha.

## GARP is a two-step ranking recipe, not one ratio

Published GARP specifications fall into three families. They disagree on both the valuation and the growth input, so the screener should make those choices explicit parameters. The **Lynch/PEG family** divides P/E by an EPS growth rate in percent. Lynch's own variant adds dividend yield to growth. AAII's Lynch screen requires the dividend-adjusted PEG to be ≤ 0.50, P/E below the industry median, 5-year EPS growth no higher than 50%, five years of positive earnings, and it excludes financials entirely ([AAII](https://www.aaii.com/latest/article/27126-former-wall-street-investment-managers-screening-strategy)). The **consensus family** (Fidelity, Zacks) uses forward numbers. Fidelity's screen requires forward P/E ≤ 15, PEG ≤ 1.0, TTM EPS growth ≥ 10%, last-quarter YoY EPS growth ≥ 5%, and projected quarterly and annual growth floors ([Fidelity](https://research2.fidelity.com/fidelity/screeners/commonstock/strategy.asp?simpleStrategy=%7BF61E8D5C-32C9-499B-8F6D-DD6DAF53EB78%7D)). Zacks uses industry-relative cutoffs: PEG and F1 P/E below the industry median, plus a **> 5% rise in the current-year EPS estimate over 4 weeks** ([Nasdaq/Zacks](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/pick-these-5-lucrative-garp-stocks-using-peg-ratio)). The **index family** avoids PEG altogether. S&P DJI's GARP indices compute a Growth z-score from 3-year EPS CAGR and 3-year sales-per-share CAGR, keep the top slice, then rank survivors on a Quality/Value z-score built from ROE, financial leverage and earnings yield ([S&P DJI methodology](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/methodologies/methodology-sp-garp-indices.pdf)).

S&P's reason for ranking in sequence is the most useful design insight in the literature. **The lowest-PEG buckets carried the highest return volatility** in US and Australian data, which dragged down risk-adjusted returns, so S&P concluded that "an index simply focusing on the PEG ratio might not be sufficient" ([S&P DJI](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/index-tv/article/understanding-garp-strategies)). Its generalised cutoffs are the **top 30% on growth, then the top half of those on QV, with a 20% selection buffer** for existing constituents ([S&P DJI paper](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/education/education-a-systematic-approach-to-garp.pdf)). The S&P 500 GARP index takes the top 150 by growth, then the top 75 by QV, weights by growth score, and caps stocks at 5% and sectors at 40%. S&P's EPS CAGR uses the **absolute value of the starting EPS in the denominator**, so a turnaround from loss to profit gives a defined number, and inputs are winsorized at the 2.5th/97.5th percentiles ([Indexology 2019](https://www.indexologyblog.com/2019/03/25/using-garp-strategies-for-indices/)). The Australian version drops leverage because of bank weight and scores on ROE and P/E only ([Global X AU](https://www.globalxetfs.com.au/insights/post/inside-garp-methodology-rules-based-strategy/)).

India has **no NSE or BSE GARP index**, but NSE publishes the components. NSE Quality is an equal-weight z-score of ROE, negative D/E and negative 5-year EPS growth variability. For financial services firms, D/E is dropped and the score is 50/50 ROE and variability. Quality eligibility requires **positive EPS in each of the previous 6 financial years**. NSE converts z-scores to positive scores with 1+Z (Z>0) or 1/(1−Z) (Z<0) ([NSE Multi-Factor Methodology](https://archives.nseindia.com/content/indices/NIFTY_Multi-Factor_Indices_Methodology.pdf)). The new **Nifty500 Growth 50** supplies the growth leg. It scores 3-year PAT growth, 3-year sales growth and 1-year risk-adjusted return, has no valuation screen, and trades at a P/E of about 41 ([NSE factsheet](https://www.niftyindices.com/Factsheet/Factsheet_Nifty500_Growth_50.pdf)).

US absolute thresholds do not transfer. The Nifty 500 traded at about **22–24x trailing earnings through 2026** ([Craytheon](https://craytheon.com/charts/nse-sector-pe/nifty-500-pe-ratio/)), so Fidelity's P/E ≤ 15 would exclude most of the universe. Unit-free rules (PEG near 1, growth floors) and relative rules (below sector median) carry over cleanly. Practitioner guidance also warns that **PEG below about 0.5 often signals over-optimistic growth forecasts** rather than a bargain ([SmartTrust](https://www.smarttrustuit.com/content/uploads/2015/01/SmartTrust_The-World-According-to-GARP_White-Paper.pdf)). Combining these sources gives the specification below. The specific Indian cutoffs are this report's synthesis; no Indian source publishes a GARP threshold set. Tags show the data each rule needs: [STMT] statements, [PRICE] OHLCV, [CONS] analyst consensus, [IDX] historical index membership.

| Stage | Rule (recommended default) | Data | Grid to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Universe | Nifty 500 members **as of the rebalance date** | [IDX] | — |

## Plain PEG has thin evidence; India's premia sit in the components

The most careful public test of GARP is FactorResearch's US study for CFA Institute (1989–2018). It defined GARP as PEG < 1 using TTM P/E over 3-year EPS growth, rebalanced monthly with 10 bps costs. About **38% of US stocks qualified**, so the screen barely selects. GARP "outperformed substantially" from 1989, but **much of the edge vanished once negative-earnings stocks were excluded**. GARP behaved like Value after 2001. With a 2000 start Value won, and with a 2010 start the broad market won ([CFA Institute](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/blogs/enterprising-investor/2019/garp-investing-golden-or-garbage)). Practitioner backtests look better but are confounded. AAII's forward-growth PEG screen made **12.9% a year vs 5.7% for the S&P 500 (1998–May 2023)**, but it also requires quarterly EPS growth and strong relative price strength. Its returns are hypothetical and price-only ([AAII](https://www.aaii.com/journal/article/223803-spotting-value-on-the-move-with-the-peg-ratio)). The plain Lynch GARP screen made about 8.0% a year over the same period.

Academic work on analyst expectations argues against putting long-term consensus growth in the PEG denominator. La Porta (1996) and Bordalo, Gennaioli, La Porta and Shleifer (2019) show that the stocks with the most optimistic long-term growth forecasts subsequently **underperform**, because analysts extrapolate past growth that then mean-reverts ([NBER](https://nber.org/papers/23863)). The lowest forward PEG is exactly where those inflated forecasts sit. Damodaran adds that there is "no basis" for calling a stock cheap just because its P/E is below its growth rate, since PEG ignores risk, payout and how long growth lasts ([NYU Stern](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/lectures/peg.htm)). The practical conclusion is to use short-horizon estimates (FY1/FY2) and revisions, not 3–5-year LTG.

India has **no large-scale academic test of PEG or GARP on the NSE universe**. The only PEG study found covers NSE mid-cap IT stocks alone and gives no sample size or significance tests ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bcp/journl/v3y2019i1p81-83.html)). Evidence for the components is strong, though. The IIMA factor library (CMIE Prowess data, survivorship-corrected) shows long-short momentum at **10.96% a year** and value (HML) at **6.86% a year** for 1993–2025, with deep drawdowns of −62% and −64%, while size was −4.01% a year ([IIMA](https://faculty.iima.ac.in/iffm/Indian-Fama-French-Momentum/drawdown.php)). Over the earlier 1993–2013 window the same library put momentum near 21–22% a year ([IIMA working paper](https://web.iima.ac.in/~iffm/legacy/four-factors-India-90s-onwards-IIM-WP-Version-original-Sep13.pdf)), so the premium has decayed but remains the strongest. Quality is unusually strong in India. Jacob, Pradeep and Varma (1995–2021) find a quality-minus-junk alpha of **0.92% a month, about twice the US estimate**, with smaller drawdowns and better behaviour in weak markets ([Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/performance-of-factor-strategies-in-india/)). Earnings revisions also worked. S&P DJI found that equal-weighted top quintiles earned **4.6–8.1% a year excess (2005–2016)**, but the effect came with high turnover and sector bias, and it **lost significance under cap-weighting** ([S&P DJI Indexology](https://www.indexologyblog.com/2018/02/21/do-earnings-revisions-matter-in-india/)).

NSE's backtested-plus-live indices since April 2005 give the most relevant benchmarks. As of 30 September 2026, the **Nifty500 Multifactor MQVLv 50 compounded at 19.09% with 17.24% volatility and beta 0.72**. The Growth 50, with no valuation filter, compounded at 18.53% with 23.59% volatility, and Quality 50 at 16.37% ([MQVLv factsheet](https://www.niftyindices.com/Factsheet/Factsheet_Nifty500MultifactorMQVLv50.pdf); [Growth 50 factsheet](https://www.niftyindices.com/Factsheet/Factsheet_Nifty500_Growth_50.pdf); [Quality 50 factsheet](https://www.niftyindices.com/Factsheet/Factsheet_Nifty500Quality50.pdf)). Most of each record is pre-launch backtest, so design-selection bias applies. A GARP composite is, in effect, a growth-plus-quality-plus-value blend, and the multifactor index is the closest existing analogue. It earned the best return per unit of risk among the indices retrieved.

Regime dependence is large, and it decides how GARP will look in a backtest. How tight the valuation cap is determines whether GARP behaves like value or like growth. The **Nifty500 Value 50 had a 5-year CAGR of −1.93% to July 2020** ([NSE factsheet 2020](https://www.niftyindices.com/Factsheet/Factsheet_Nifty500_Value50.pdf)), then **34.50% for the five years to December 2025** ([NSE Dashboard Dec 2025](https://www.niftyindices.com/Index_Dashboard/Index_Dashboard_DEC2025.pdf)). In the 12 months to September 2026, with Nifty 50 at −7.09%, Growth 50 returned +9.42% and Value 30 +7.89%, while Quality 30 fell 5.42% ([NSE Dashboard Sep 2026](https://www.niftyindices.com/Index_Dashboard/Index_Dashboard_SEP2026.pdf)). A GARP sleeve with a tight cap (PEG < 1 or P/E below sector median) should therefore look like value: strong in 2021–25, weak in 2015–20. Loosening the cap toward PEG 2 moves it toward the Growth 50 profile, with beta near 1.3. Neither version has been tested directly on NSE, so the user's own backtest will be the first real evidence.

## No cheap point-in-time data exists, so design the backtest around lags

No free, terms-of-service-clean, programmatic source provides 10+ years of NSE fundamentals. yfinance returns only about **4 years of annual statements** for `.NS` tickers ([TradingQnA](https://tradingqna.com/t/what-are-api-for-getting-fundamental-data-for-stocks-trading-in-nse-india-free-or-paid/145292)). That is enough for a live screener's trailing metrics but not for a backtest. Screener.in advertises 10+ years, but its licence is "personal, non-commercial transitory viewing only" and forbids copying and mirroring ([Screener.in Terms](https://www.screener.in/guides/terms)). It also shows restated rather than as-reported figures. NSE's data-portal terms bar "systematic or automated data collection, including scraping" without written consent ([NSE Terms](https://dotexdata.nseindia.com/TermsAndConditions/TermsofUse.pdf)). That rules out bulk-harvesting the exchange XBRL filings, which would otherwise be the ideal source. Those filings are timestamped in their filenames, so each carries a known availability time ([NSE archive example](https://nsearchives.nseindia.com/corporate/DSML_14022026190347_Integrated_Filing__Financial_docx.pdf)). The terms risk matters more because the user's separate fund-website project could put derived data on public display.

The realistic paid routes are global APIs and institutional databases. **EODHD** offers annual and quarterly statements, analyst "Earnings Trend" estimates with revisions, and splits. Crucially, each report carries both a period `date` and a **`filing_date`**. Non-US history reaches back to 2000, but often only 6 years and 20 quarters for smaller companies. Fundamentals plans start around $59.99 a month ([EODHD docs](https://eodhd.com/financial-apis/stock-etfs-fundamental-data-feeds)). Its NSE depth across the Nifty 500 is **unverified**, and the same is true of FMP. Trial both against a handful of large-, mid- and small-cap tickers before committing. **CMIE Prowess**, the database behind the IIMA factor library, covers roughly 38,000–51,000 Indian companies and is the research-grade option, but it is sold to institutions with unpublished pricing ([IIM Calcutta library](https://library.iimcal.ac.in/cmie-prowessiq/)). For consensus estimates, Trendlyne's Forecaster covers **about 900 companies**, roughly the Nifty 500, but it has no confirmed public API ([Trendlyne help](https://help.trendlyne.com/support/solutions/articles/84000383175-what-are-forecaster-or-analyst-estimates-)). Historical consensus for a forward-PEG or revisions backtest is only well documented in Bloomberg, LSEG I/B/E/S and FactSet.

Because the data is restated rather than point-in-time, the reporting lag carries most of the defence against look-ahead bias. SEBI's Integrated Filing (Financial) rules require **quarterly results within 45 days of quarter-end for Q1–Q3 and annual audited results within 60 days of year-end** ([MSEI circular](https://www.msei.in/SX-Content/Company-Circulars/2025/August/EXCHANGE-04-AUG-2025-IMPLEMENTATION-OF-RECOMMENDATIONS-OF-THE-EXPERT-COMMITTEE-FOR-FACILITATING-EASE-OF-DOING-BUSINESS-FOR-LISTED-ENTITIES---INTEGRATED-FILING.pdf)). For a March fiscal year, that puts the deadlines near **14 August, 14 November, 14 February and 30 May** ([Patron Accounting](https://www.patronaccounting.com/glossary/accounting/sebi-financial-reporting-timelines)). Make each record usable from the next trading day after its vendor `filing_date`, capped by the deadline. Where no filing date exists, use the deadline itself. Use a 90-day lag for the COVID window, because FY20 results were allowed until 31 July 2020 ([LegalMantra](https://legalmantra.net/blog-detail/SEBI-EXTENDED-TIMELINE-FOR-SUBMISSION-OF-FINANCIAL-RESULTS-ENDED-ON-31ST-MARCH2020-TILL-31ST-JULY-2020)). Restatements and consolidation changes still leak into history, and a backtest report should say so. Survivorship is the larger risk: IIMA explicitly corrects for it because "many Indian public companies have disappeared" ([IIMA working paper](https://web.iima.ac.in/~iffm/legacy/four-factors-India-90s-onwards-IIM-WP-Version-original-Sep13.pdf)). The universe must be historical Nifty 500 membership, including delisted names, not today's constituent list.

Several Indian data-hygiene rules follow, and they are standard practice rather than sourced findings. Use consolidated figures consistently and record the basis on each row, since LODR requires consolidated results where subsidiaries exist ([TaxGuru](https://taxguru.in/sebi/sebi-listing-regulations-financial-results-annual-reportinformation.html)). Bonuses are common in India, so adjust historical EPS with the same cumulative factor that already adjusts the OHLCV cache, which keeps P/E on one share basis. Flag share-count jumps above 20% that have no split or bonus behind them, since they usually mark mergers or rights issues. Use PAT before exceptional items where the vendor separates them. Align TTM windows by quarter-end date, not by fiscal-year label.

## A separate quarterly GARP sleeve beats bolting a filter onto swing trades

The four candidate designs differ mainly in where they expect the edge to come from. Fundamental premia accrue over months and are measured with monthly to annual rebalancing. Over a 5–40 day window, price noise swamps them. Earnings-announcement drift, the short-horizon fundamental effect, lasts about two months in India ([IIMB, Kudalkar & Chandra](https://repository.iimb.ac.in/handle/2074/20501)). Even that is contested: a 2014–18 study of 100 NSE firms found no exploitable drift and results that varied by liquidity ([Gupta & Dhusia](https://journals.lib.vutbr.cz/index.php/trends/article/view/541)). Indian costs push in the same direction. **STT alone is 0.1% on each side**, plus stamp duty of 0.015% on buys and exchange charges ([StockCalc 2026](https://stockcalc.in/blog/stock-broker-charges-india-2026)), so a round trip costs at least about 0.25% before slippage. Short-term capital gains are also taxed above long-term gains (rates should be verified against the current Finance Act). Both favour holds of several months.

| Design | Holding | Fit to evidence | New capabilities required |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(A) Pure GARP sleeve** (recommended first build) | 3–12 months; quarterly refresh | Best: matches how premia are measured; low turnover | Fundamentals pipeline with filing dates; PIT Nifty 500 constituents; portfolio rebalancer (target weights, rank buffer, name/sector caps); fundamental sell rules; portfolio-level backtest engine |
| **(B) GARP watchlist + technical entry** (second build) | Open-ended, trailing stop, re-qualify each results season | Good: India's strongest premium is momentum, and value–momentum diversify | Everything in A except the rebalancer; watchlist refresh job; trailing-stop exits with no fixed TP; hold limit tied to results calendar |
| **(C) Event-driven results trigger** | ~1 results cycle (~60 trading days) | Mixed Indian PEAD evidence; useful as timing anchor | Results-calendar feed (NSE board-meeting and results announcements); quarterly EPS history for a time-series SUE |
| **(D) GARP filter on existing 5–40 day swings** | 5–40 days | Weakest: GARP acts only as a junk filter | Quarterly fundamentals snapshot only |

For **design A**, the S&P and NSE conventions translate into the following reference specification. Apply the gates and two-step ranking from the table above, and hold the **top 25–30 names**, equal-weighted or growth-score-tilted, with a **5% name cap** and a **25–30% sector cap**. The cap matters because PEG screens over-weight financials and consumer discretionary ([CFA Institute](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/blogs/enterprising-investor/2019/garp-investing-golden-or-garbage)). Rebalance fully twice a year: in mid-June, once annual results are in, and mid-December. Run quarterly exception checks in mid-March and mid-September. NSE's own 2026 study found that quarterly rebalancing added **20–50% more churn** for at most a marginal gain over semi-annual ([NSE momentum whitepaper](https://www.niftyindices.com/docs/default-source/indices/nifty200-momentum-30/momentum-strategy-whitepaper_2026.pdf)). Keep incumbents until their rank falls below 2N, mirroring S&P's 20% buffer and NSE's rule that excludes a holding only when its rank drops below 50. Between rebalances, sell on hard breaks only: TTM EPS turning negative, EPS growth below the floor for two consecutive quarters, or trailing PEG above 2. An optional catastrophic stop (200-day MA break or −25%) can sit on top. These sell rules are practitioner convention and untested, so they belong in the parameter grid. A regime overlay can reuse the existing breadth filter to trim small-cap weight when the Nifty Smallcap 250 is below its 200-day MA. Small caps carry real liquidity risk: SEBI's stress tests showed large small-cap funds needing **up to about 60 days to sell half their portfolios** ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/finance/personal-finance/explained-should-small-cap-funds-stress-test-results-worry-you-124031800197_1.html)).

**Design B** is the natural bridge to the existing tool and the CAN SLIM/SEPA tradition. A quarterly GARP watchlist (positive TTM EPS, TTM EPS growth ≥ 15–20%, sales growth ≥ 10–15%, PEG ≤ 1.5, ROE ≥ 12–15%, D/E ≤ 1) feeds a daily technical gate. The gate requires a trend template (price above the 50/150/200-day MAs and near the 52-week high), top-tercile sector rank and breadth on. Entries then come from the existing triggers: trendline bounce, breakout, MA pullback, or a results-day gap on at least 2× volume with a positive time-series SUE. Exits replace the fixed take-profit with a trailing MA or ATR stop. Add a hard exit when a held name posts a negative surprise, since Indian drift is strongest after bad news ([Nottingham](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/26569)), and re-qualify fundamentals each results season. The case for this pairing rests on value and momentum being negatively correlated, about −0.60 for stocks ([Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~lpederse/papers/ValMomEverywhere.pdf)). NSE already runs a similar quantamental index, Nifty500 Multicap Momentum Quality 50 ([NSE press release](https://nsearchives.nseindia.com/web/sites/default/files/2024-09/ind_prs04092024.pdf)). Hard evidence that fundamental-screen-plus-technical-timing beats the fundamental screen bought and held is absent, though. Peer-reviewed CAN SLIM evidence amounts to one 2.5-year paper-trading test ([Lutey & Rayome](https://mail.articlegateway.com/index.php/JAF/article/view/5134)).

In the Gemini rule compiler, the GARP rules split into two kinds. Gates and growth floors are filters ("TTM EPS growth at least 15%"), which compile naturally to per-stock boolean predicates once fundamentals sit beside OHLCV. Ranking, z-scoring, sector-relative P/E, rank buffers and caps are **cross-sectional and portfolio-level operations** that a per-symbol daily-bar rule language cannot express. They belong in verified Common Method Library functions, such as `growth_z`, `qv_z`, `sector_relative_pe` and `rebalance_with_buffer`, that the compiler calls rather than generates. Validation should use coarse grids (PEG 1.0/1.5/2.0; growth floors 15/20/25%), require stable results across neighbouring settings, and run walk-forward windows covering 2008, the 2018–19 small-cap bear market, 2020 and 2024–25. Results should be reported by market-cap bucket and sector, and benchmarked against Nifty 500 TRI, MQVLv 50 and Growth 50. For design B, also compare against the same technical system without the GARP filter, which isolates the filter's actual contribution.

## Conclusion

The research changes what "building GARP" means for FrontierTradeLight. The ratio everyone quotes, PEG, is the least defensible part of GARP. In India it becomes useful only when ranked inside a growth-plus-quality-plus-value composite whose components each carry a documented premium. Quality is especially strong in India. So the right first deliverable is the infrastructure, not a rule: filing-dated fundamentals, historical Nifty 500 membership and a portfolio rebalancer. The same infrastructure would also support quality, value and multifactor sleeves at little extra cost, so GARP is a reasonable first tenant for a fundamental platform rather than a one-off strategy.

Two uncertainties should be settled before paying for anything large. First, trial EODHD or FMP on 20 tickers across market-cap tiers to confirm the depth of 10-year history and the accuracy of `filing_date` against NSE announcement timestamps. That single check decides whether a credible backtest costs about $60 a month or needs an institutional licence. Second, set expectations correctly. A tight-valuation GARP sleeve will probably look like a lower-beta value strategy, a loose one like the Growth 50, and the choice of cap is effectively a choice of regime bet. Running both variants side by side in the backtest, rather than tuning one, is the most honest way to learn which exposure the user actually wants.
