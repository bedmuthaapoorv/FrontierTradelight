# Building a real signal and duration model for Event Watcher

Event Watcher's current signal — a single `_price_signal()` comparison of pre- and post-event closing price against a hardcoded 10% threshold, with no magnitude, no confidence, and no duration estimate — is a rule-of-thumb, not a calibrated trading signal, and the academic and vendor literature both explain exactly why that gap matters and how to close it. The professional playbook, from MacKinlay's classic event-study framework to RavenPack's and Bloomberg's production sentiment engines, converges on a two-layer design: an **event-type prior** (a base-rate expectation calibrated from that category's own historical outcomes) adjusted by a **story-specific likelihood** (deal size, sentiment, volume confirmation), producing both a magnitude estimate and a confidence score rather than one static label. On duration, there is no single number to borrow from the literature — post-earnings drift runs **about 60 trading days**, analyst-recommendation drift fades in **1–4 weeks**, buyback drift can run **12–48 months** ([Wikipedia: PEAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift); [Talval: analyst upgrades](https://talval.com/learn/do-analyst-upgrades-beat-the-market)) — and contract/order-win announcements specifically have **no published longer-horizon drift studies at all**, only same-day reaction figures. That means Event Watcher's duration model has to be built in-house from its own historical event log, using tools that already exist in this codebase (`generated_exit_strategy.py`'s day-by-day walk, `backtest.py`'s trade-outcome table) plus one new library, `lifelines`, for proper survival-style duration estimation. The rest of this report lays out the methodology and then translates it into concrete changes to `Strategies/Default/event_watcher_utils.py`.

## Event studies build a score from a return baseline, not a single price comparison

The academic starting point is MacKinlay's event-study framework: estimate a stock's "normal" expected return from a pre-event **estimation window** (commonly ~120 trading days) via a market-model regression against an index, then measure the **abnormal return** — actual minus expected — over a short **event window** around the announcement, summed into a cumulative abnormal return, or CAR ([MacKinlay 1997](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:jeclit:v:35:y:1997:i:1:p:13-39)). This is the crucial thing `_price_signal()` skips entirely: it treats *any* price rise after the event as "the signal," when a rise that simply tracks a rallying Nifty or a rallying defense sector is not abnormal at all. A genuinely useful score needs a benchmark-adjusted return, not a raw price delta — for PSU/defense names this likely means benchmarking against a sector index (Nifty PSE or a defense-basket proxy) rather than Nifty 50, since sector beta for these names tends to be high and idiosyncratic within the broader market ([backtesting_calibration.md notes](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/20-05.pdf)).

On top of that abnormal-return baseline, no vendor or academic source publishes a single disclosed composite formula — RavenPack's Composite Sentiment Score and Event Sentiment Score, Bloomberg's Market Moving News probabilities, and AlphaSense's confidence-weighted sentiment are all proprietary, empirically calibrated systems, not closed-form equations ([RavenPack CSS](https://www.ravenpack.com/research/composite-sentiment-score/); [Bloomberg trading the news](https://www.bloomberg.com/professional/insights/data/trading-news-use-machine-readable-data-find-alpha/); [AlphaSense sentiment methodology](https://help.alpha-sense.com/hc/en-us/articles/41711605431059-How-We-Calculate-Sentiment)). But their architecture is consistent and reusable: RavenPack's finding that CSS (linguistic sentiment) and ESS (event-category classification) are only weakly correlated (Pearson r=32.6%) but jointly informative — a neutral-sentiment story still resolves 76.2% of the time to positive or negative once its **event category** is known — is the strongest evidence that a taxonomy-driven prior (what kind of event is this) and a story-specific adjustment (what does this particular story say) are separate, complementary signal components, not one blended number ([RavenPack](https://www.ravenpack.com/research/composite-sentiment-score/)). Distributional evidence backs this up directly: post-event CAR distributions differ sharply in dispersion and tail shape by event type — risk-warning events are wide and heavy-tailed, routine dividend/industry announcements cluster near zero ([Janus-Q, arXiv 2602.19919](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.19919)) — meaning a single global score, applied identically whether the news is a contract win or a routine filing, is statistically indefensible on its face.

For contract/PSU-award announcements specifically, the two academic studies located both report modest, same-day effects: a broad corporate-contracting study found **1.43% average abnormal return for contractors** on announcement day generally, and **0.54%, still statistically significant, for corporate-government contracts specifically** ([UCLA Anderson working paper](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/20-05.pdf)); a pharma-sector event study (12 NASDAQ firms, 327 event-days, ±10-day CAPM-based window) found a significant *cumulative* reaction across the window even though individual daily abnormal returns were not always significant ([Anser Press](https://www.anserpress.org/journal/jea/3/1/43)). Anecdotal Indian examples (BHEL +1.74%, RVNL ~+2.4% on large order wins) are directionally consistent but are news reports, not peer-reviewed studies ([Zee Business](https://www.zeebiz.com/markets/stocks/news-rvnl-share-price-nse-bse-multibagger-psu-stock-to-buy-rail-vikas-nigam-limited-order-win-from-indian-railway-299485); [Tradebrains](https://tradebrains.in/240000-cr-order-book-psu-stock-jumps-after-securing-21000-cr-power-project-contract/)). The magnitude of reported contract-win effects (roughly 0.5–1.5%) is comparable to average M&A acquirer CAR (~1.1%) ([Bhagat firm-size study](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/bhagat/acquisitiongains-firmsize.pdf)) but with weaker, more context-dependent significance — a real signal for this event class must normalize for **relative deal size** (contract value ÷ market cap or ÷ average daily volume), since that ratio, not the announcement itself, is what appears to drive whether a reaction is meaningful. Critically, no peer-reviewed India-specific NSE/BSE study of PSU/defense contract announcements exists; Event Watcher cannot import an external base rate for this event category and must calibrate its own from its own event log.

## No literature answers "how long," so duration has to be estimated, not imported

The distinction between *magnitude* and *duration* turns out to be close to independent in the literature, and that independence is itself the most important finding for Event Watcher's design. Classic post-earnings-announcement drift (PEAD) work (Bernard & Thomas 1989/1990) documents drift persisting roughly **60 trading days** (about one quarter), with **25–30% of the total drift concentrated in the 3-day windows around the next 1–3 quarterly earnings releases** even though those windows are only ~5% of the elapsed trading days — evidence that drift is not a smooth decay but re-accelerates around subsequent information events ([Wikipedia: PEAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift)). Notably, the *magnitude* of the PEAD anomaly has shrunk substantially since the 1980s/90s as it has been arbitraged away, while the *duration* shape has stayed roughly quarter-length — meaning a duration estimate does not need to be re-derived every time the magnitude estimate is recalibrated; they can be modeled as separate quantities updated on separate cadences.

Other event types span a wide duration range: analyst-recommendation drift is short and front-loaded, **1–4 weeks**, essentially fully dissipated (median excess return of exactly zero) by three months ([Talval](https://talval.com/learn/do-analyst-upgrades-beat-the-market)); buyback-announcement drift is the opposite extreme, running **12–48 months** post-announcement, itself weakening for post-2001 announcements versus pre-1994 ones as that anomaly too gets partly arbitraged ([ECGI working paper](https://www.ecgi.global/sites/default/files/working_papers/documents/finalmanconipeyervermaelen_0.pdf); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0929119919309368)); M&A arbitrage spreads are qualitatively different again — they converge to zero on the deal's closing timeline (45 days for a tender offer, multiple years for a regulator-heavy merger), driven by discrete milestones (antitrust clearance, shareholder vote) rather than a diffusing decay ([Verdad: Merger Arbitrage](https://verdadcap.com/archive/merger-arbitrage)). Contract/order-win studies, by contrast, almost universally stop at a **±10 trading-day event window** and report nothing about longer-horizon persistence ([Anser Press](https://www.anserpress.org/journal/jea/3/1/43)) — this is a genuine gap in the published literature, not a search-completeness problem, and it is precisely the event type Event Watcher cares about most.

A useful cross-check, though a distinct quantity, comes from options markets and volatility modeling. GARCH-model volatility half-life is a well-defined, computable formula — **half-life = ln(0.5) / ln(α + β)** — where α governs sensitivity to new shocks and β governs persistence of prior variance; illustrative instrument-level half-lives run around **42 trading days for the S&P 500**, **38 for Treasuries**, and **16 for oil**, all instrument-specific rather than universal ([Ryan O'Connell CFA: EWMA/GARCH](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/volatility-estimation-garch/)). This measures how long a stock stays "jumpy" after a shock, not how long its price keeps trending in one direction — a real but separate concept from directional drift duration, and options markets' own term-structure behavior (an inverted vol curve around a scheduled event collapsing quickly afterward) implicitly assumes most single-news shocks are short-lived even though the equity literature shows directional drift can persist for weeks to months — a tension worth carrying into the design rather than resolving by picking one framework. Ranking every duration figure found, from shortest to longest: contract-win reaction (~10 days studied, unknown beyond that) < analyst-action drift (1–4 weeks) < GARCH volatility half-life (16–42 days) < PEAD (60–90 days) < momentum (3–12 month formation/hold, partial reversal by 12–18 months) < buybacks (12–48 months) ([synthesis across sources above]). This ordering is a reasonable starting prior table, not a substitute for measuring contract-win duration directly from Event Watcher's own history.

The right formal tool for estimating that duration empirically is **survival analysis**, not a fixed-window average — and one directly transferable academic precedent exists. A study modeling "time from a stock index's peak until it falls 20% from that peak" used exactly the machinery needed here: Kaplan-Meier survival curves (treating indices that haven't yet declined by the end of the sample as **right-censored**, not as zero-duration observations), a logistic model for probability of reaching the threshold, and a Cox proportional-hazards model for the *speed* of decline conditional on covariates ([PMC: survival analysis of stock index decline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7968564/)). Swap "20% decline from peak" for "price reverts N% from its post-event extreme," swap the paper's region/continent covariates for event-type/sector/deal-size, and the entire pipeline transfers essentially unchanged. In Python, `lifelines` is the standard implementation: `KaplanMeierFitter` for the non-parametric survival curve and its `median_survival_time_`, and `WeibullAFTFitter` for a parametric model whose `predict_expectation()` / `predict_percentile(df, p=0.5)` give a single expected/median "days to reversion" number while `predict_survival_function()` gives the full decay curve — both from one fitted model, and both natively handling **right-censoring** for events whose swing hasn't reverted yet by the time the model is queried ([lifelines WeibullAFTFitter docs](https://lifelines.readthedocs.io/en/latest/fitters/regression/WeibullAFTFitter.html); [Towards Data Science survival-analysis guide](https://towardsdatascience.com/a-survival-analysis-guide-with-python-using-time-to-event-models-to-forecast-customer-lifetime/)).

## Small samples force shrinkage, not naive per-stock averages

Corporate contract wins are rare events at the single-stock level — a handful of instances per company per year at most — so any per-stock, per-event-type historical average will be dangerously noisy if used raw. The statistically correct remedy, well-established since Efron & Morris's development of the **James–Stein estimator**, is empirical-Bayes shrinkage: pull a noisy small-sample estimate toward a more reliably estimated group mean, with the pooling weight determined by the data itself (relative sample sizes and between-group variance) rather than a hand-picked constant. James–Stein provably dominates the naive sample mean under squared-error loss whenever three or more related groups are estimated jointly, and its generalization — hierarchical Bayesian partial pooling, fit via tools like Stan or PyMC — is already used in finance for exactly this kind of short-sample estimation problem (equity factor-beta estimation) ([Berkeley course notes on James-Stein](https://stat210a.berkeley.edu/fall-2024/reader/jamesstein.html); [Stan hierarchical partial pooling case study](https://mc-stan.org/learn-stan/case-studies/pool-binary-trials.html)). The natural three-level hierarchy for Event Watcher is: **stock × event-type** (noisiest, fewest observations) shrinking toward **sector × event-type** (e.g., all PSU/defense names' contract-win reactions pooled) shrinking toward **event-type alone** (broadest, most stable) — directly analogous to the classic empirical-Bayes baseball batting-average problem, where a player's early-season average is pulled toward the league mean in proportion to how few at-bats they've had ([in-season batting-average empirical Bayes, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0803.3697)).

Two specific statistic types need two different shrinkage recipes. A **win rate** (fraction of historical contract-win instances with positive abnormal return) is a proportion bounded in [0,1], so it is naturally suited to **Beta-Binomial conjugate Bayesian updating**: `Prior: p ~ Beta(α₀, β₀)`, and after observing x successes out of n new instances, `Posterior: p | x ~ Beta(α₀+x, β₀+n−x)`, with posterior mean `(α₀+x)/(α₀+β₀+n)` — a weighted average of the prior and the observed rate, entirely closed-form, no MCMC library required ([MetricGate: Bayesian binomial update](https://metricgate.com/docs/bayesian-binomial-beta-update/)). Continuous, typically skewed statistics — median CAR, days-to-peak, days-to-50%-reversion — are better handled with median/IQR robust statistics (since CAR distributions are documented as heavy-tailed for some event types) and Normal-based or Normal-Inverse-Gamma conjugate updating, which handles unknown mean *and* unknown variance simultaneously and updates in closed form as each new event resolves ([NIG conjugate prior](https://dirknbr.medium.com/a-simple-bayesian-rl-with-normal-inverse-gamma-updates-f00977cd0beb)). No source found a ready-made shrinkage formula specifically for a *duration* statistic — this needs to be adapted ad hoc, most plausibly by working in log-duration space and applying the same Normal-conjugate logic, since duration is a right-skewed positive quantity. EWMA (with RiskMetrics' industry-standard decay λ=0.94 as a default) is a simpler, non-Bayesian fallback for online tracking that naturally down-weights stale regime data and requires no derivation — a reasonable first implementation before a fuller conjugate-Bayesian version is built ([EWMA overview](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/exponentially-weighted-moving-average)).

Backtesting this kind of rare-event, small-sample signal also has to guard against the standard event-driven-backtest failure modes: **look-ahead bias** (using information that would not have been available at decision time, which produces backtest-vs-live gaps that are "immediate and catastrophic" rather than gradual) and **survivorship bias** (building the historical universe from today's current index constituents, silently excluding names that delisted along the way) ([Look-Ahead Bias in Backtesting](https://algorier.com/blog/look-ahead-bias-in-backtesting/); [Look-Ahead-Freedom paper, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.04958)). For a rare-event calibration specifically, the standard fix for low statistical power is not "collect more days of data" but "collect more event *instances*" — pooling across stocks and sectors, since power in rare-event designs is governed by event count, not raw observation count ([rare-events sample-size discussion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.13671)).

## Composite scoring: a transparent weighted z-score sum with a confidence gate

No vendor discloses an explicit weighting formula — RavenPack, Bloomberg, and AlphaSense all treat their composite construction as proprietary IP, calibrated empirically rather than derived from a closed-form model. That is itself useful information: a transparent, hand-inspectable linear composite is a legitimate design choice for Event Watcher not because it's industry practice, but because it trades a little predictive power for interpretability and debuggability, which matters more for a small internal tool than for a hedge fund's alpha engine. Two implementable patterns recur across adjacent domains. The **z-score approach**, standard in quant multi-factor investing (Quantpedia-style composite ranking): normalize each factor to `z = (x − mean) / std` over a rolling lookback, then take a weighted average across factors into one composite z-score ([Z-Score Normalized Linear Signal](https://medium.com/@redsword_23261/z-score-normalized-linear-signal-quantitative-trading-strategy-a3a3b073c0cc)). The **credit-scorecard approach**, standard in credit risk: transform each factor via Weight-of-Evidence (`WOE = ln(% non-events / % events)` per bin), fit a logistic regression on the WOE-transformed variables, and convert the fitted coefficients into a points scorecard — this has the added benefit of a built-in, well-tested rule for missing/inapplicable factors (renormalize by dividing the sum of applicable-factor scores by the sum of applicable weights) and yields a calibrated probability, not just a rank ([ListenData: WOE and IV](https://www.listendata.com/2015/03/weight-of-evidence-woe-and-information.html)). Given Event Watcher's likely small labeled-event count, the z-score route is the safer starting point; WOE/logistic regression becomes worthwhile once enough resolved historical events accumulate to fit coefficients without overfitting.

A two-number output — a **magnitude score** and a separate **confidence score** — maps directly onto the **meta-labeling** architecture from quantitative finance practice (López de Prado): a primary model estimates direction/magnitude, and a secondary model estimates the probability that this call will be realized, which is then used to size or gate the trade rather than fire it uniformly ([Hudson & Thames: meta-labeling](https://hudsonthames.org/meta-labeling-a-toy-example/)). For Event Watcher, the "primary model" is the event-type's historical base-rate CAR (the prior), and the "secondary/confidence model" is a function of sample size, relative deal size, and how much the specific story's attributes deviate from that event type's typical pattern.

## Concrete redesign for this codebase

The current implementation of the signal lives entirely in one function: `_price_signal(symbols, event_date_str)` in `Strategies/Default/event_watcher_utils.py` (around line 163), which pulls the last close before the event and the current close, computes a raw `pct_move`, and maps it to one of three static strings — `HOLD` if flat/down, `BUY` if up but under the hardcoded `SIGNAL_RALLY_ALREADY_PLAYED_OUT_PCT = 10.0` (line 154, justified only by a Cochin Shipyard anecdote), `SELL` if up 10%+. This should be replaced, not patched, with a function that returns a structured composite result instead of a label:

- **Benchmark-adjust the return first.** Instead of comparing raw close-to-close, compute an abnormal return against a sector benchmark (Nifty PSE or a defense-basket index) over the same window — this directly fixes the biggest conceptual flaw (a stock rallying with its sector reads as a false "signal" today).
- **Add an event-type dimension.** `events.csv`'s `MatchedKeyword` column is already a rough taxonomy (e.g. contract-signing vs. other categories) — use it (or a slightly refined keyword-to-event-type mapping in the existing keyword-config file loaded by `_load_keyword_config()`) as the bucket key for a per-event-type historical distribution, mirroring the literature's finding that CAR dispersion and tail shape differ materially by event type.
- **Replace the three static labels with a composite score object**, e.g. `{magnitude_score: float, confidence: float, expected_car_pct: float, label: str}`, where `magnitude_score` is a weighted z-score combination of (a) the event-type's historical median abnormal return (the "prior," shrunk toward the sector/event-type hierarchy described above), (b) relative deal size (contract value ÷ market cap, when extractable from the announcement title/text — currently unused data), and (c) a volume-confirmation z-score if volume data is available; `confidence` is derived from the event-type bucket's sample size (`n_samples`) and its Beta-Binomial win-rate posterior, exactly the meta-labeling split of "how big" vs. "how sure." The `label` (BUY/HOLD/SELL) can still be derived from thresholds on `magnitude_score`, preserving the existing UI contract in `Strategies/Default/api/routers/event_watcher.py` and `Strategies/frontend/src/pages/EventWatcher.tsx` while making the underlying number meaningful instead of a single 10% cutoff.

For duration, there is currently no logic in Event Watcher at all — this needs a **new function**, not a patch to an existing one. Two files already in this codebase contain reusable skeletons for exactly this: `generated_exit_strategy.py`'s `resolve_exit()` / `describe_state()` walk a position day-by-day, tracking `days_held`, a trailing-ATR stop, and a hardcoded `MaxHold` cutoff at 90 days (line 55: `if days_held >= 90: return {"ExitDate": ..., "ExitReason": "MaxHold"}`); and `backtest.py`'s `_forward_return_pct()` (line 331) computes a return over a fixed `NEAR_MISS_HOLDING_DAYS = 20`-day window and feeds a `trades_df` with `EntryDate`/`ExitDate`/`ExitReason` columns (built around line 444) — but nothing today aggregates `ExitDate − EntryDate` across trades into a duration distribution. The concrete plan:

1. **Add an aggregation step that reuses `backtest.py`'s `trades_df` schema.** For every historical event-triggered trade, compute `duration_days = (ExitDate − EntryDate).days` alongside the existing `ExitReason`, and treat `ExitReason == "MaxHold"` (or any trade still open when the historical data window ends) as **right-censored** rather than as a completed observation — this is the single most important correction to make, since naively averaging only *closed* trades systematically understates true duration by dropping the longest-running ones.
2. **Feed that censored duration data into `lifelines`.** A new function, e.g. `estimate_swing_duration(event_type: str) -> DurationEstimate`, would fit a `WeibullAFTFitter` (or start simpler with `KaplanMeierFitter` for just a median survival time) on the historical trades filtered to that event type, using `event_col` = 1 for genuinely reverted/exited trades and 0 for `MaxHold`/still-open ones. `aft.predict_median()` or `predict_expectation()` gives the single "expected days until this swing plays out" number Event Watcher's UI needs; `aft.predict_survival_function()` gives the full decay curve if the frontend ever wants to plot it.
3. **Track a per-event-type `EventTypeDistribution` object** (median CAR, CAR IQR, Beta-Binomial `alpha`/`beta` win-rate state, Weibull duration params, `n_samples`, `last_updated`) that both the score and the duration function read from and that gets refreshed on a batch cadence (nightly/weekly), since neither the market-model regression nor the Weibull AFT fit are natively incremental — the Beta-Binomial win-rate update is the only piece that is genuinely O(1) per new event.
4. **Backfill the historical distribution from `events.csv` itself.** Since `events.csv` already stores `Date`, `MatchedSymbols`, and `MatchedKeyword` for every past match, a one-time script can reconstruct historical `EntryDate`/price-at-entry pairs for every past event, run them through the same day-by-day walk pattern as `resolve_exit()` (with a wider `MaxHold`, e.g. 90–120 days matching PEAD-scale horizons, rather than 20 days, since contract-win drift duration is unknown and needs to be measured rather than assumed short) and produce the first real `EventTypeDistribution` for the "contract-signing" bucket — turning the module's own admitted "never been backtested" docstring into an honest, calibrated one.

## Conclusion

The redesign this research points to is less about a cleverer formula and more about a structural fix: separate what Event Watcher currently conflates. Today one function does three jobs badly — measuring reaction, judging its significance, and implicitly assuming duration — with a single hardcoded threshold. The literature's clearest lesson is that these should be three explicit, separately calibrated pieces: a benchmark-adjusted magnitude estimate shrunk from sparse per-stock data toward a sector/event-type prior, a confidence score derived from that same sample size, and a duration estimate built from survival analysis on the module's own historical trade outcomes rather than assumed to match earnings or M&A timelines it structurally differs from. The most consequential and actionable single finding is that contract/order-win drift duration has never been published anywhere — Event Watcher's own historical `events.csv`, reprocessed through the existing `resolve_exit()`/`backtest.py` machinery and a `lifelines` Weibull/Kaplan-Meier fit, is not a nice-to-have cross-check but the *only* available source of truth for that number, and building it turns Event Watcher from a heuristic into the kind of internally-validated, reusable signal the rest of the literature assumes as a starting point.
