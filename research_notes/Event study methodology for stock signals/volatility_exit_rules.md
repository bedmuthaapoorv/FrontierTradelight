# Volatility-Grounded Position-Exit Frameworks (ATR stops, expected-move horizons, optimal stopping, trend-following exits)

## What is the theoretical/practical justification for using ATR or realized volatility to size stop-loss and trailing-stop distances, and what ATR multiples are commonly recommended?

### Takeaway
ATR-based stops are justified as a way to normalize stop distance to an instrument's own "noise level" so that a stop is unlikely to be hit by ordinary price chop rather than a genuine trend reversal; the specific multiples in wide use (2N for initial stops, 3x ATR for trailing/chandelier stops) come from Wilder's original heuristic design and from Richard Dennis/William Eckhardt's Turtle system, and are described in the literature as practical/empirical conventions rather than results derived from a closed-form optimization.

### Cited Findings
- J. Welles Wilder Jr. introduced Average True Range (and the related Volatility System, Parabolic SAR, RSI, and ADX) in his 1978 book *New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems* — [Average True Range (ATR) | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-indicators/average-true-range-atr); [J. Welles Wilder Jr.: The Father of Modern Technical Analysis Indicators](https://www.marketopia.org/blog/j-welles-wilder-jr/)
- Wilder's standard ATR smoothing period is 14 (daily bars), and this has become the de facto industry-standard lookback for volatility measurement across charting platforms — [Average True Range (ATR) | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-indicators/average-true-range-atr)
- Wilder's original Volatility System (also from the 1978 book) used an ATR multiplier of 2.8–3.1 applied to a 7-period ATR to set stop distance — [Chandelier Exit - Definition, Formula, Calculate, Use](https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/equities/chandelier-exit/) (search-result synthesis citing Wilder's Volatility System)
- The Chandelier Exit — a volatility trailing stop developed by Charles "Chuck" Le Beau and popularized by Alexander Elder — is computed as: Long stop = 22-day High − 3 × ATR(22); Short stop = 22-day Low + 3 × ATR(22). Le Beau's recommended defaults are a 22-period lookback (≈ one trading month) and a 3x ATR multiplier — [Chandelier Exit | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-overlays/chandelier-exit)
- StockCharts' rationale for the 3x multiplier: it creates "a buffer that is three times the volatility" so that only a price move substantial relative to the recent volatility regime triggers an exit; documentation explicitly notes multipliers should be tuned per-instrument (higher multiplier / wider stop for more volatile names like tech stocks to reduce false stop-outs, tighter multiplier for calmer names) — [Chandelier Exit | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-overlays/chandelier-exit)
- Unlike the Chandelier Exit (trails from the rolling highest-high/lowest-low), Wilder's own Volatility System trails the stop from the close, making it tighter/more responsive than Le Beau's later variant — [ATR Trailing Stop Guide: Chandelier Exit & Volatility | StratBase.ai](https://stratbase.ai/en/blog/average-true-range-trailing-stop) (search-result synthesis)
- In the Turtle Trading system (Richard Dennis and William Eckhardt, 1983 experiment), volatility is measured as "N" (essentially a 20-day ATR), and every position's stop-loss is placed at "2N" (2x ATR/N) from the entry price; position size is set so that a 1N adverse move equals 1% of account equity, so a 2N stop caps initial risk at 2% of equity per unit — [Turtle Trading Strategy: Richard Dennis Rules, Statistics, and Backtests](https://www.quantifiedstrategies.com/turtle-trading-strategy/); [Richard Dennis Turtle Trading Strategy & Rules Explained](https://trendspider.com/learning-center/richard-dennis-turtle-trading-strategy/)
- Van Tharp's percent-volatility position-sizing model (popularized to retail traders in the late 1990s, building on the Turtles' approach) explicitly recommends using ATR to measure a stock's volatility, basing the stop-loss distance on a multiple of ATR (example given: 2x ATR) specifically so the stop sits "outside the normal noise of the market," and then deriving position size from that ATR-based stop distance so that dollar risk per trade is held constant regardless of the instrument's volatility — [The Ultimate Guide to Van Tharp's Position Sizing Strategies](https://quantstrategy.io/blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-van-tharps-position-sizing-strategies/); [Van Tharp 7 - Stops and Exits - 7 Circles](https://the7circles.uk/van-tharp-7-stops-and-exits/)
- The general ATR-based position-sizing formula used across this literature: Position size = (Account equity × Risk%) ÷ (ATR × Stop multiple) — this makes position size shrink automatically when volatility rises and grow when volatility falls, holding dollar risk per trade roughly constant across instruments and regimes — [Average True Range: Dynamic Stop Loss Levels | LuxAlgo Blog](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/average-true-range-dynamic-stop-loss-levels/) (search-result synthesis)
- Van Tharp (and separately Tom Basso) frame the professional convention as risking roughly 0.5%–2% of total account equity per single trade, for long-run survival/ruin-avoidance reasons rather than a return-optimization derivation — [The Art and Science of Position Sizing: Lessons from Van Tharp and Tom Basso](https://tradingmomentum.substack.com/p/the-art-and-science-of-position-sizing)

### Inferences
- The "2x/3x ATR" convention functions as a practical noise filter: ATR approximates the typical bar-to-bar price swing, so a stop several ATRs away is, by construction, unlikely to be hit by routine intraday/day-to-day volatility alone — it requires a move that is large relative to the stock's own recent behavior, which is the intended interpretation of "volatility-adjusted" rather than "fixed-percentage" stops.
- There is a consistent design split in the literature between a *tighter* trailing multiple (Wilder's original ~2.8–3.1x on a short 7-day ATR, or Turtles'/Van Tharp's 2x ATR for the *initial* hard stop) and a *wider* multiple for trend-following trailing/chandelier stops (Le Beau's 3x on a 22-day ATR) — the tighter setting protects capital on a fresh position, the wider setting is meant to let a working trend "breathe" without being stopped out on normal pullbacks. This suggests the "right" multiple is a function of *purpose* (initial risk cap vs. profit-protection trail) rather than a single universal constant.
- None of the standard multiples (2N, 2x ATR, 2.8–3.1x, 3x ATR) are presented in primary sources as derived from a formal statistical optimization (e.g., minimizing whipsaw rate subject to a fixed false-stop probability); they are practitioner heuristics validated by long-run track records (Wilder's own trading, the Turtles' documented results, Le Beau/Elder's discretionary+systematic trading), which is consistent with the "broadly validated across markets, not curve-fit to one dataset" framing but is not the same as a closed-form theoretical guarantee.

### Gaps
- No primary-source (Wilder's own book text) was retrievable in this pass to directly quote his stated rationale for the 2.8–3.1 multiplier; this is reported second-hand through synthesized search-result summaries (StratBase.ai, Corporate Finance Institute) rather than the original 1978 text — treat the exact multiplier value as reasonably well-corroborated across multiple secondary sources but not independently primary-verified here.
- Could not find a rigorous statistical derivation (e.g., a false-stop-probability calculation assuming ATR ≈ a given fraction of daily standard deviation) connecting a specific ATR multiple to a specific confidence level; the multiples appear to be conventions, not derived confidence-interval widths.

## What does the options-pricing / volatility literature imply about a "reasonable" horizon over which a stock is expected to move a given number of standard deviations — can volatility be used to derive a principled expected time-to-target?

### Takeaway
Because returns are commonly modeled as following a random walk / geometric Brownian motion, return variance scales linearly with time and standard deviation (and hence "expected move") scales with the square root of time — this gives a direct, closed-form way to convert an ATR or historical/implied volatility estimate into an expected number of days to reach a given price target, rather than picking a holding period arbitrarily.

### Cited Findings
- The standard options-market "expected move" formula for a one-standard-deviation range over horizon T is: Expected Move = Stock Price × IV × √(T / 365) — [Expected Move in Options: How to Calculate and Trade It](https://volatilitybox.com/research/expected-move-options/); consistent formula also given by [Deribit Insights](https://insights.deribit.com/education/expected-move-calculations-and-visual/) and [projectoption Expected Move Calculator](https://projectoption.com/expected-move-calculator)
- Worked example: with IV = 20% and stock price = $100 at 30 days to expiration, EM = 100 × 0.20 × √(30/365) ≈ $5.72, i.e., a ±1σ range of roughly $94.28–$105.72 — [Expected Move in Options: How to Calculate and Trade It](https://volatilitybox.com/research/expected-move-options/) (search-result synthesis)
- This one-standard-deviation range corresponds to roughly a 68% probability that price stays within it over the given horizon, following the standard normal/lognormal assumption behind the calculation — [Standard deviation and expected move](https://optionalpha.com/community/posts/standard-deviation-and-expected-move-2025052911532) (search-result synthesis)
- The square-root-of-time scaling is a direct mathematical consequence of the random-walk/Brownian-motion assumption: if daily returns are independent and identically distributed, their variances add linearly over time, so standard deviation (and therefore expected move) over N days equals √N times the one-day standard deviation — [Standard deviation and expected move](https://optionalpha.com/community/posts/standard-deviation-and-expected-move-2025052911532) (search-result synthesis); consistent with the general random-walk model cited in options-education material at [Understanding the 'Rule of 16' in Plain Terms](https://www.optionseducation.org/news/understanding-the-rule-of-16-in-plain-terms)
- A key practical implication: expected move does NOT scale linearly with time — a 30-day expected move is not twice the 15-day expected move, but rather √2 (≈1.41×) as large — [Expected Move in Options: How to Calculate and Trade It](https://volatilitybox.com/research/expected-move-options/) (search-result synthesis)
- The "Rule of 16" is a practitioner shortcut for converting between annualized volatility and one-day expected move: daily expected move (1σ) ≈ annualized IV ÷ 16, because 16 ≈ √252 (the number of trading days in a year); e.g., 32% annualized IV implies roughly a 2% expected daily move — [Understanding the 'Rule of 16' in Plain Terms](https://www.optionseducation.org/news/understanding-the-rule-of-16-in-plain-terms); [Volatility Rule of 16 - Macroption](https://www.macroption.com/volatility-rule-of-16/)
- The Rule of 16 can be run in reverse to annualize an observed short-horizon move: e.g., observed daily moves around 1.8% imply an annualized volatility of roughly 1.8 × 16 ≈ 28.8% — [Understanding the 'Rule of 16' in Plain Terms](https://www.optionseducation.org/news/understanding-the-rule-of-16-in-plain-terms)

### Inferences
- This gives a principled, closed-form way to derive an "expected time-to-target" for an event-driven signal without a large local backtest sample: given a stock's ATR (as a proxy for daily σ) or its implied volatility, and a target price move of k × ATR (or k standard deviations), the expected number of trading days T to reach that move (under a random-walk/no-drift assumption) can be backed out by inverting Expected Move = σ_daily × √T, i.e., T ≈ (target move / σ_daily)². This lets a time-based exit rule be derived from the same volatility input used to size the stop, rather than being picked as an arbitrary fixed number of days (e.g., "exit after 5 days") independent of the instrument's volatility regime.
- Because expected move scales as √T rather than T, a stop set at k × ATR implicitly corresponds to a roughly consistent "days to typically reach or fail to reach" horizon across different volatility regimes for the same stock — high-volatility stocks reach a fixed ATR-multiple target faster in calendar time than the same multiple would for a low-volatility stock, but the number of "volatility units" traveled is held constant. This is the conceptual bridge between ATR-based stop *distance* and a statistically grounded *time* component for the same exit rule.
- Caution: the random-walk/GBM assumption underlying √T scaling is a modeling convenience, not a guaranteed description of any specific stock's actual return process — event-driven / news-driven moves in particular are known to exhibit autocorrelation, jumps, and volatility clustering that violate the i.i.d. assumption underlying the √T rule. The rule is best treated as a first-order approximation for horizon-setting, not an exact prediction.

### Gaps
- Could not find a specific academic or practitioner source explicitly proposing "invert the expected-move formula to derive an event-driven trade's expected holding period" as a named, established technique — this is a reasonable and standard mathematical inference from the cited expected-move / random-walk literature, but it is presented here as an inference, not a directly cited established practice under that name.
- Did not find literature specifically addressing how the presence of a known volatility-clustering/jump component around news events (e.g., GARCH effects, earnings-jump volatility) should adjust the naive √T expected-move calculation; this would need dedicated research into event-study volatility dynamics (likely covered by another workstream on event-study methodology) rather than the general volatility-scaling literature reviewed here.

## Are there established "optimal stopping" or sequential-decision frameworks from probability/finance theory giving closed-form or semi-analytical guidance on exiting a position whose edge decays over time?

### Takeaway
Optimal-stopping theory has been applied formally to trailing-stop and buy/sell timing problems under diffusion models (GBM and mean-reverting alternatives), but the results are cautionary rather than prescriptive for a simple rule of thumb: under a pure GBM with positive drift, a trailing stop is not obviously the optimal exit rule, and rigorous solutions typically require numerical methods or a richer model (e.g., mean-reversion, or a trailing stop combined with a limit order) rather than yielding a simple closed-form "exit at X" formula that generalizes cleanly to signal-decay situations.

### Cited Findings
- Academic work has modeled trailing-stop trading problems using geometric Brownian motion and exponential Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (mean-reverting) price processes within a general linear-diffusion optimal-stopping framework — [Optimal Trading with a Trailing Stop (arXiv:1701.03960)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.03960); also indexed at [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386633418_Optimal_Trading_with_a_Trailing_Stop) and [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00245-019-09559-0)
- A notable finding in this literature: it would be optimal to *never* use a trailing stop if the stock followed a GBM with positive drift and no other constraints — i.e., under the simplest "signal never decays, drift is constant and known" model, a trailing stop is not the value-maximizing exit rule; stopping-time theory in that idealized setting instead favors either an infinite horizon hold or a different exit trigger entirely — [Optimal Trading with a Trailing Stop | Request PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386633418_Optimal_Trading_with_a_Trailing_Stop) (search-result synthesis)
- The same line of research finds that as volatility increases, the optimal liquidation (sell) trigger level rises (you demand a bigger favorable move before locking in gains) due to the stronger random push from Brownian motion, while the optimal acquisition (buy) level falls (you're willing to enter at a lower level) — [Optimal Trading with a Trailing Stop | Request PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386633418_Optimal_Trading_with_a_Trailing_Stop) (search-result synthesis)
- The arXiv paper's own abstract indicates the authors work in "a general linear diffusion framework" (not narrowly GBM) and derive that combining a trailing stop *with* a sell limit order is the jointly optimal strategy for timing both entry and exit, rather than a trailing stop alone — [arXiv:1701.03960 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.03960)
- Separately, "buy low, sell high" has been formalized as an optimal-stopping problem, but the relevant reward/value function lacks the smoothness needed for the standard free-boundary (smooth-fit) solution technique, forcing reliance on non-standard or numerical analysis rather than a clean closed form — [An Optimal Stopping Problem of Detecting Entry Points for Trading Modeled by Geometric Brownian Motion](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10614-019-09915-w) (search-result synthesis)
- More broadly, the literature search confirms that optimal-stopping analyses of trailing stops "often require numerical methods rather than closed-form solutions" once realistic features (drift uncertainty, mean-reversion, combined order types) are introduced — (search-result synthesis across multiple papers indexed under this query)

### Inferences
- The theory does NOT support treating a fixed-percent or fixed-ATR-multiple trailing stop as provably "optimal" in a strict expected-utility sense, even under the cleanest textbook model (GBM); it is better understood as a robust, easy-to-implement heuristic that approximates good stopping behavior without requiring the trader to know/estimate the true drift, which real optimal-stopping solutions typically need as an input. This is an important nuance for a signal-decay framing: if a signal's "edge" (excess expected drift) is understood to decay over time, that is closer to the mean-reverting / time-varying-drift models this literature flags as requiring numerical solution, not a case where a single closed-form multiple can be derived.
- The result that volatility increases push the optimal *sell* trigger further away (not closer) is a useful corrective if one's intuition says "higher volatility should mean a tighter stop to limit risk" — the sequential-decision-theory result instead says a rational discretionary exit level widens with volatility because of the added noise/opportunity cost of stopping out on transient swings. This is broadly consistent with the ATR-scaling design in stop-loss practice (wider stop in points for a higher-ATR stock) but for a different underlying reason (avoiding stopping out on noise vs. maximizing expected value of the stopping rule).

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve full readable text of the arXiv paper (PDF returned as corrupted/binary content in fetch) so the precise closed-form or semi-analytical formulas (e.g., an explicit optimal trailing percentage as a function of σ and drift μ) could not be extracted or verified directly — only the abstract/search-result-level summary was available. A researcher with direct PDF-parsing capability should re-attempt to extract the specific formulas from this paper (arXiv:1701.03960, "Optimal Trading with a Trailing Stop") for exact analytical results.
- No source in this search specifically modeled a "signal that starts with positive edge and decays to zero over time" as an optimal-stopping problem with an explicit closed-form holding-period result; the literature found addresses stationary-drift GBM or mean-reverting-price models, not a time-decaying-edge model. This is a real gap — a time-decaying-signal optimal-stopping formulation, if it exists in the academic literature, was not located in this pass and would need a more targeted search (e.g., "optimal stopping decaying drift", "signal decay trading horizon stopping time").

## How do momentum/trend-following CTA and systematic trading literature typically define holding-period/exit rules meant to generalize across instruments?

### Takeaway
The academic time-series-momentum literature (Moskowitz, Ooi, Pedersen) and CTA/Turtle-style systematic practice converge on volatility-scaled, rule-based exits rather than instrument-specific fitted parameters: academically, momentum/trend persistence is documented empirically to run for roughly 12 months before partially reversing over the following 1–2 years, informing standard lookback/holding-period choices; in practice, CTAs size and exit positions using ATR/volatility-normalized rules (e.g., Donchian-channel breakout exits, ATR-multiple stops) precisely because those rules are unitless (in "volatility units") and so transfer across very different instruments (currencies, commodities, bond futures, equity indices) without re-fitting.

### Cited Findings
- Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen (2012) document time-series momentum ("trend") using a diversified set of 58 futures and forward contracts across equity indices, currencies, commodities, and sovereign bonds from 1985–2009, showing that a security's own past 12-month excess return positively predicts its future return — [Time Series Momentum (NYU Stern preprint)](https://w4.stern.nyu.edu/facdir/lpederse/papers/TimeSeriesMomentum.pdf); [Time Series Momentum Effect - Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/time-series-momentum-effect)
- The original TSMOM factor construction uses a 12-month formation/lookback period with a 1-month holding period before rebalancing/re-assessing the signal, applied uniformly across all instrument classes in the study (not tuned per instrument) — [Time Series Momentum Effect - Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/time-series-momentum-effect)
- The trend/momentum effect is found to persist for about 12 months and then partially reverse over the subsequent roughly 1–2 years (timing of reversal found to occur 12–24 months after portfolio formation), which is explicitly noted as a shorter reversal window than traditional cross-sectional momentum reversals (typically cited at 2–5 years) — [Time series momentum - ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X11002613); search-synthesis on reversal timing citing the same body of work
- The authors report this result is robust "across a number of sub-samples, look-back periods and holding periods," which is the paper's own evidence that the 12-month/1-month framing is not narrowly fit to one dataset — [Time series momentum - ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X11002613) (search-result synthesis)
- On the practitioner/CTA side, Michael Covel's documentation of the Turtle system describes a strategy combining simple, mechanical Donchian-channel breakout entry/exit rules with ATR-based ("N") volatility position sizing and broad diversification across many futures markets, credited with producing meaningful long-term returns using the same rule set unmodified across very different instruments — [Turtle Trading Strategy: Richard Dennis Rules, Statistics, and Backtests](https://www.quantifiedstrategies.com/turtle-trading-strategy/) (Covel's documentation referenced in search synthesis)
- General CTA/trend-following practice is described as combining a signal filter (moving averages, breakout, Donchian channels) with systematic ATR/volatility-based stop-loss and position sizing so that risk per trade is normalized across instruments of very different price/volatility scales — [Trend following trading - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trend_following_trading); [CTA Strategies - DayTrading.com](https://www.daytrading.com/cta-strategies)

### Inferences
- The design principle shared by both the academic TSMOM literature and CTA practice is expressing exit/holding-period rules in *volatility-normalized* or *return-based* units (percent excess return over 12 months; ATR multiples for stops; Donchian channel breakout of the N-period high/low) rather than in raw price or calendar terms alone — this is exactly what allows the same rule to be applied unmodified to instruments as different as bond futures and commodities, and is the generalizable design pattern to borrow for a single-stock, event-driven exit rule: define the exit threshold in units of the stock's own ATR/realized volatility, not a fixed rupee/dollar amount or an arbitrarily chosen number of days.
- The empirical ~12-month persistence/reversal finding is itself instrument-agnostic evidence (documented across 58 different contract types) that trend signals have a finite, roughly-consistent "half-life" in calendar time at the asset-class level; for an event-driven single-stock signal (much shorter-lived than a 12-month macro trend), this suggests it is reasonable, by analogy, to expect the informational edge of a news event to decay within a bounded, volatility-scaled window rather than persisting indefinitely — though the specific numerical horizon for news-driven single-stock signals would need separate event-study-specific research (outside this workstream's scope).
