# Post-Announcement Price Drift and Evidence-Based Optimal Holding Periods

## How long does PEAD persist after an earnings surprise, and does it generalize to non-earnings announcements?

### Takeaway
The classic PEAD literature (Ball & Brown 1968; Bernard & Thomas 1989/1990) documents that abnormal returns following an earnings surprise drift in the direction of the surprise for at least 60 trading days (roughly one calendar quarter), with the largest share of the drift concentrated in the first ~20 trading days and a long tail that can extend 60-90+ days; the effect is earnings-specific in its original design and only partially generalizes to other event types via separate, distinct literatures (momentum, M&A, contract awards, FDA approvals) rather than a single unified "drift" theory.

### Cited Findings
- Bernard and Thomas (1989) found a positive (negative) drift of about 2% over 60 trading days for good- (bad-) news stocks following quarterly earnings announcements; firms with good earnings news see abnormal returns drift upward for at least 60 days post-announcement, and bad-news firms drift downward for a similar period — [Bernard & Thomas 1989, Journal of Accounting Research](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/joares/v27y1989ip1-36.html)
- The spread in average returns between top- and bottom-decile SUE (standardized unexpected earnings) portfolios was positive in 41 of 48 quarters from 1974-1985, and in 11 of 16 quarters when the NYSE index itself was negative, indicating the drift is not simply a risk-premium artifact tied to market direction — [Bernard & Thomas 1989 summary](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=769769)
- Academic reviews describe the strongest component of PEAD occurring in the first 20 trading days post-announcement, after which the effect gradually attenuates but often remains statistically significant out to 60-90 days; some earlier studies found drift persisting across multiple subsequent quarters, though more recent research focuses mainly on the quarter directly following the announcement — [A review of the Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214635020303750)
- Chan, Jegadeesh & Lakonishok (1996) found that both past price momentum and past earnings-surprise ("SUE") momentum each independently predict large drifts in future returns even after controlling for the other, and found little evidence of subsequent reversal in stocks exhibiting high combined price-and-earnings momentum, consistent with markets responding only gradually (underreacting) to new information — [Chan, Jegadeesh & Lakonishok 1996, Journal of Finance](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1996.tb05222.x)
- Ball & Brown (1968) is credited as the origin of the anomaly, establishing that stock prices continue to drift in the direction of an earnings surprise well after the earnings figure is public, contradicting a strict efficient-market prediction of instantaneous full adjustment — [Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift); [PEAD review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214635020303750)

### Inferences
- The PEAD literature is earnings-specific by construction (it uses standardized unexpected earnings, SUE, as the surprise metric); its ~60-90 trading day drift window is the most rigorously quantified "optimal holding period" benchmark in the academic literature, but it should not be applied uncritically to non-earnings announcements without a separate evidentiary basis for each event type (see next sections).
- The fact that the bulk of the drift accrues in the first ~20 trading days (about 4 trading weeks / roughly one calendar month) suggests that even within PEAD, a shorter holding window (3-4 weeks) may capture a disproportionate share of the total drift relative to holding the full quarter.

### Gaps
- No source found that directly extends the Bernard & Thomas SUE-based drift measurement methodology to non-earnings "surprise" events (e.g., a standardized "contract-award surprise" magnitude); the generalization across event types is inferred by analogy from separate, independent literatures rather than a single study bridging both domains.

## Is there research specifically on drift after contract-award/order-win or regulatory/defense-procurement announcements? What analogous event types have documented drift/reversal timelines?

### Takeaway
Dedicated academic literature on drift specifically after contract-award or defense-procurement announcements is sparse and mostly focused on the announcement-day/short-window reaction rather than multi-week drift; the most directly relevant study (military contract awards around 9/11) measures abnormal returns over short-run (day -1 to +1) and long-run (day -1 to +60) windows, implying researchers in this niche treat ~60 trading days as the relevant long-run measurement horizon, mirroring the PEAD convention. Better-documented analogous event types — M&A announcements and FDA drug approvals — show a similar pattern: a sharp announcement-day/next-day reaction followed by drift or reversal unfolding over months to years rather than being reducible to a single day-count.

### Cited Findings
- A study of military contract award announcements around 9/11 measured abnormal returns over a short-run window (day -1 to +1) and a long-run window (day -1 to +60), explicitly using the 60-trading-day post-announcement horizon (the same horizon convention as classic PEAD) to test whether contracts were "overfunded" or "underfunded"; findings showed positive abnormal returns on the announcement day both before and after 9/11, with no significant short-run difference between the two periods, but contracts granted after 9/11 appeared "underfunded" when judged by long-run (60-day) abnormal returns — [Palkar, Larson & Larson (2012), Applied Financial Economics](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09603107.2011.607126)
- Other academic work in this space (e.g., on inter-corporate business contracting announcements, and pharmaceutical-industry public-contract-winning announcements) exists but full-text access was not obtainable during this research pass (PDF fetch failed / content not extractable); their existence indicates an active but small research niche, not a large consensus literature — [Investor Reaction to Inter-Corporate Business Contracting, UCLA Anderson](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/4-07.pdf); [Effect of Public Contract Winning Announcements on Share Prices, pharmaceutical industry](https://www.anserpress.org/journal/jea/3/1/43)
- For M&A announcements: target-firm abnormal returns are positive and significant on the announcement day and the following day in cash-financed deals; acquiring-firm abnormal returns tend to be near zero in cash deals but negative in stock-financed deals. Separately, a well-documented long-horizon drift/reversal exists: Agrawal, Jaffe & Mandelker (1992) and Loughran & Vijh (1997) found significant negative abnormal returns to stock-financing acquirers persisting even five years post-takeover, i.e., a multi-year fade rather than a multi-week one — [Event Study: M&A and its Impact on Stock Market](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/event-study-ma-its-impact-stock-market-haohan-wang); summary citing Agrawal/Jaffe/Mandelker and Loughran/Vijh findings from the same search result set
- For FDA drug approvals: the clearest price effects are concentrated in a short event window, commonly the announcement day through the next few trading days (0 to +5 trading days); one study found a statistically significant average abnormal return of 0.35% (t = 2.89) on the announcement day, with much of the reaction happening even before the official approval as the market prices in expectations ahead of time — [Market response to FDA announcements, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S106297690600007X); summary of event-window findings from search results
- Separately, on innovation announcements more broadly (patents and FDA approvals), one study found that beyond the immediate announcement effect there is a subsequent stock-return drift, and that the magnitude of the initial announcement effect increases while the magnitude of the subsequent drift decreases as investor attention to the event increases — implying attention is the key variable governing how much of the reaction happens immediately vs. over a drift period — [Chemmanur, Li, Tseng & Wang, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4519055)
- Companies' stock prices moved (small or large increase) in response to approximately 77% of FDA approval announcements in one study, underscoring that the announcement-day reaction is the dominant, most reliable component of the FDA-approval event, more so than a guaranteed subsequent drift — [Journal of Finance and Accountancy, FDA drug approval study](http://www.aabri.com/manuscripts/182804.pdf)

### Inferences
- Across contract-award, M&A, and FDA-approval literatures, researchers consistently use a short window (roughly ±1 to +5 trading days) to capture the "fast" announcement reaction and a much longer window (60 trading days for contract awards / years for M&A) to test for drift or reversal — there is no consensus "few weeks" middle-ground holding period documented specifically for contract-type announcements; the two horizons that do have empirical support are "within about a week" (fast full pricing, more typical of FDA approvals) and "about a calendar quarter, i.e. ~60 trading days" (the PEAD/military-contract convention for long-run drift measurement).
- Because dedicated contract-award drift research is thin and mostly focused on determining valuation "fairness" (over/underfunding) rather than trading-strategy optimal holding periods, an evidence-based holding period for contract/order-win trades is better anchored to the PEAD 60-trading-day (~3 month) convention (as the closest analogous, most rigorously measured discrete-news drift horizon) than to M&A (multi-year, dominated by financing-method effects) or FDA approvals (mostly a fast <1-week reaction with attention-dependent drift).

## What do event studies say about the shape of the reaction — fast full pricing within 1-3 days vs. drift over weeks/months — and what is the typical magnitude decay curve?

### Takeaway
Event studies broadly document a two-part shape: a large, sharp abnormal return concentrated in the announcement day and immediately adjacent day(s), followed by a smaller-magnitude but statistically persistent drift that decays gradually rather than reversing sharply; the specific decay curve is best quantified for earnings (concentrated in the first ~20 trading days, tapering out through 60-90 days) while for FDA approvals the fast component (0 to +5 days) dominates and the drift component is attention-dependent (larger investor attention → bigger immediate reaction, smaller residual drift, and vice versa).

### Cited Findings
- For earnings announcements: the strongest component of PEAD occurs in the first 20 trading days, after which the effect "gradually attenuates but remains statistically significant" out to about 60 days, and some measurements extend the drift to 60-90+ days — [PEAD review, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214635020303750)
- For FDA drug approvals: "the clearest price effects are concentrated around a short event window — commonly the day of the announcement and the next few trading days (0 to +5 trading days)," and "much of the reaction often occurs before the official approval" as the market prices in expectations ahead of the event — search result summary of FDA event-study literature
- For innovation announcements (patents/FDA approvals) generally: the magnitude of the immediate announcement effect increases, and the magnitude of the subsequent drift decreases, as investor attention to the event rises — meaning the "decay curve" shape is not fixed but is itself a function of how much attention the specific announcement receives — [Chemmanur, Li, Tseng & Wang, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4519055)
- For price momentum more broadly (Jegadeesh & Titman 1993): buying past winners and selling past losers generated significant positive returns over 3- to 12-month holding periods, averaging about 1% per month; testing 16 formation/holding-period combinations (3, 6, 9, 12 months each) all produced positive returns, with the strongest combination being a 12-month formation period paired with a 3-month holding period (about 1.31%/month) — indicating that at the multi-month momentum horizon (distinct from the days-to-weeks PEAD/event-study horizon), returns continue to accrue for up to about a year before decaying — [Jegadeesh & Titman 1993, Journal of Finance](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1993.tb04702.x)

### Inferences
- There appear to be at least two distinct "drift" phenomena operating on different timescales that should not be conflated: (1) a days-to-~quarter event-specific drift (PEAD, contract-award, FDA) driven by underreaction/limited attention to a discrete piece of news, and (2) a multi-month (3-12 month) price/earnings momentum effect (Jegadeesh-Titman, Chan-Jegadeesh-Lakonishok) that is a cross-sectional portfolio phenomenon built from many past-return signals rather than a single discrete announcement. For a single discrete contract/order-announcement trade, the shorter (days-to-quarter) event-study literature is the more directly applicable analogy; the momentum literature is a useful upper bound/sanity check on how long a "drift" of any kind has ever been shown to persist before decaying (about 12 months) before the well-documented reversal phase begins.
- The shape across all event types studied is consistently "front-loaded": a large abnormal return in the first 1-5 days captures a large fraction of the total eventual move, with the remaining drift being a smaller, decaying tail rather than a second wave of comparable magnitude.

### Gaps
- No source found that provides a precise mathematical decay curve (e.g., percentage of total drift captured by day N) specifically for contract/order-win or defense-procurement announcements; the "20 trading days captures most of it" figure is sourced from earnings-announcement literature only and is being used here as the best available analogy, not a directly measured figure for non-earnings announcements.

## What does the literature say about the point of "reversal," and what explanations (underreaction, limited attention, risk premia) are offered for both the initial drift and its fade?

### Takeaway
For price momentum (the best-documented multi-month drift/reversal cycle), abnormal returns typically begin to reverse around 12 months after formation and dissipate within about two years; for PEAD specifically, the classic finding is drift without a clean documented reversal date within the studied window (60-90 days), and the anomaly itself is reported to have weakened over time in more recent samples. Two main classes of explanation compete in the literature: behavioral underreaction/limited-attention (investors are slow to process news, causing initial drift, which then closes as attention/arbitrage catches up) versus risk-premium explanations (the drift is compensation for earnings-surprise risk, growth risk, or liquidity risk, not mispricing).

### Cited Findings
- In the Jegadeesh-Titman momentum framework, part of the abnormal returns generated in the first year after portfolio formation dissipates over the following two years, and momentum premiums are not permanent — they start experiencing negative abnormal returns around 12 months after formation and dissipate within two years — [Momentum research summary citing Jegadeesh & Titman](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2018/preliminary/paper/6ABQfdEY); [Jegadeesh & Titman 1993](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1993.tb04702.x)
- Chan, Jegadeesh & Lakonishok (1996) explicitly found "little evidence of subsequent reversals" in stocks with high combined price-and-earnings momentum, and argued market risk, size, and book-to-market factors do not explain the drift, favoring a gradual/underreaction interpretation over a pure risk-based one — [Chan, Jegadeesh & Lakonishok 1996](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1996.tb05222.x)
- The PEAD anomaly has been reported to weaken in recent decades; one line of research argues this is due to "arbitrage trading" competing the anomaly away post-publication, but an alternative explanation is that the underlying persistence of earnings surprises from quarter to quarter has itself declined, meaning there is simply less to anticipate/underreact to, independent of arbitrage — [PEAD weakening discussion, search-aggregated academic literature](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1057521924003922)
- Behavioral/underreaction explanation: limited investor attention and distraction from extraneous news inhibit market reactions to relevant earnings news (the "investor distraction hypothesis"); a higher number of same-day earnings releases makes surprises harder to process, and announcements made on Fridays (lower attention days) show higher PEAD — consistent with underreaction being attention-driven and fading as attention normalizes — [Driven to Distraction, Hirshleifer et al., Journal of Finance 2009](https://sites.uci.edu/dhirshle/abstracts/driven-to-distraction-extraneous-events-and-underreaction-to-earnings-news/)
- Risk-premium explanation: one study found the well-documented positive PEAD is present only when risk premia are high, which the authors argue is "consistent with a risk explanation of the PEAD rather than one due to information delay" — directly contradicting the underreaction camp — [Risk premium PEAD research, search-aggregated](https://afajof.org/management/viewp.php?n=55300)
- Multifactor risk models have been constructed adding an earnings-surprise risk factor to the Fama-French three-factor model, which "provides a remarkable improvement in explaining post-earnings announcement drift," and separate work has proposed "expected growth risk" (covariance of returns with future real GDP growth) as a driver, with expected growth risk rising with standardized unexpected earnings — [A Multifactor Explanation of Post-Earnings Announcement Drift, JFQA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/multifactor-explanation-of-postearnings-announcement-drift/59BBDF0CCAC18EBD491E6511C6EDB705)

### Inferences
- The literature is genuinely split (not resolved) between underreaction/behavioral and risk-premium explanations for PEAD; this matters practically because if drift is a risk premium, it should persist indefinitely as compensation and not "fade" in a systematic knowable way, whereas if it is underreaction, it should fade as attention/arbitrage catches up — the empirical fact that PEAD has weakened over recent decades is more consistent with (at least partial) underreaction/limited-attention plus arbitrage competition than with a pure, stable risk premium.
- The clearest, most concretely dated "reversal point" in the literature is the momentum literature's ~12-month mark, not a PEAD-specific reversal date; no source reviewed gives a specific day/week at which earnings-driven drift itself reverses (as opposed to just decaying/flattening) within the 60-90 day PEAD window.

### Gaps
- No source found specifying an exact "reversal" date for PEAD itself (as distinct from decay/attenuation) — the literature reviewed describes the drift flattening out by 60-90 days but does not document a subsequent negative reversal phase specific to earnings surprises within the horizons studied.
- No contract-award-specific or litigation-specific reversal research was found; reversal timing evidence is drawn entirely from the momentum and PEAD literatures and should be treated as an analogy, not a direct finding, when applied to contract/order-win or litigation-settlement trades.
