# Quantitative Methods for Estimating Post-Event Price Swing / Drift Duration

## What does the PEAD literature say about how long drift persists after an earnings surprise, and what duration windows are typically studied?

### Takeaway
Classic PEAD studies (Bernard & Thomas 1989/1990) document drift persisting roughly one full quarter (~60 trading days), with the drift heavily concentrated (25-30%) in narrow 3-day windows around the next quarterly earnings release; more recent work confirms drift out to 60-90+ days but shows the magnitude has shrunk substantially since the 1980s/1990s as the anomaly has partly been arbitraged away.

### Cited Findings
- Bernard & Thomas (1989) found a positive (negative) drift of about 2% over 60 trading days for good- (bad-) news stocks following an earnings announcement — the classic ~60-trading-day (roughly one-quarter) drift window — [Bernard & Thomas 1989 discussion / earnings-watcher summary](https://earnings-watcher.com/wiki/post-earnings-drift-pead/)
- Bernard & Thomas (1990) found that approximately 25-30% of total post-earnings-announcement drift occurs during the three-day windows surrounding the subsequent (next 1-3) quarterly earnings announcements, even though those windows represent only about 5% of total trading days in the drift period — [Wikipedia: Post-earnings-announcement drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift)
- Bernard & Thomas (1990) zero-investment (long high-SUE / short low-SUE) portfolios generated 8-9% abnormal returns over one quarter (~35% annualized before transaction costs), rising to about 67% annualized when the holding period is extended through the subsequent earnings announcement — [Wikipedia: PEAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift)
- Bernard & Thomas (1990) report the autocorrelation structure of quarterly seasonally-differenced earnings/SUE-related returns decays across lags: first lag ≈0.34, second lag ≈0.19, third lag ≈0.06, fourth lag ≈ -0.24 — a pattern consistent with a decaying-then-reversing drift over roughly four quarters — [Wikipedia: PEAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift)
- Livnat et al. (2005) document PEAD persisting for "at least 60 days" following the earnings announcement — [Wikipedia: PEAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift)
- Ball & Brown (1968) and later Hirshleifer et al. (2008) describe the drift qualitatively as lasting "several weeks (even several months)" post-announcement — [Wikipedia: PEAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift)
- Garfinkel et al. (2024) find a hedge portfolio (long top standardized-unexpected-earnings [SUE] decile, short bottom decile) generates a 5.1% risk-adjusted return over three months (~20% annualized), with historical annual range 8.76%-43.08% depending on sample period — [Wikipedia: PEAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift)
- Kettell et al. (2022) show the high-SUE/low-SUE return spread has declined over time, from roughly 5% in the 1980s/1990s to ≤3% by the late 2010s, indicating the anomaly's magnitude (though apparently not its qualitative duration) has been shrinking — [Wikipedia: PEAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift)
- In practice, momentum in the drift is strongest during the first one to ten sessions after the earnings print, then flattens as the surprise is progressively priced in — [Earnings-watcher PEAD summary](https://earnings-watcher.com/wiki/post-earnings-drift-pead/)

### Inferences
- The literature converges on a "quarter-length" (~60 trading day) canonical duration for PEAD, but the drift is not smooth — it decays in the interim and then re-accelerates sharply around the next 1-3 earnings dates, so a single decay-curve/half-life model may understate the periodic re-acceleration; a systematic Event Watcher module estimating "duration" for earnings-type events should probably default to ~60 trading days (about one quarter) as a primary duration estimate, with awareness that a meaningful fraction of the remaining move will re-trigger around the next earnings date.
- The consistent finding that the *magnitude* has decayed over decades (post-1990s arbitrage) does not appear to change the *duration* shape much — this suggests duration (how many days a move takes to play out) and magnitude (how big the move is) may be quasi-independent quantities in this literature, an important distinction for any duration-only model.

### Gaps
- No source found gives an explicit formal "half-life" (in the exponential-decay sense) fitted to the PEAD CAR curve; the closest analog is the autocorrelation-of-returns figures from Bernard & Thomas (1990), which are quarter-level (not daily) and would need reinterpretation to produce a daily half-life.
- Could not access the original Bernard & Thomas 1989/1990 papers directly (paywalled); reliance is on secondary summaries (Wikipedia, earnings-watcher.com), which should be cross-checked against the primary papers (Bernard, V., & Thomas, J. (1989). "Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift: Delayed Price Response or Risk Premium?" Journal of Accounting Research, 27, 1-36; and Bernard, V., & Thomas, J. (1990). "Evidence That Stock Prices Do Not Fully Reflect the Implications of Current Earnings for Future Earnings." Journal of Accounting and Economics, 13(4), 305-340) if higher precision is required.

## What is known about drift/momentum persistence after other event types: M&A announcement drift, contract/order-win announcements, analyst upgrade/downgrade drift, buyback announcements?

### Takeaway
Non-earnings event drifts vary widely in duration: analyst-recommendation drift is short (front-loaded over 1-4 weeks, largely gone by ~3 months), buyback-announcement drift is very long-horizon (12-48 months), M&A arbitrage-spread convergence tracks the deal-closing timeline (45 days to multi-year, driven by regulatory/vote milestones rather than a decay curve), and contract/order-win announcement studies mostly examine short symmetric event windows (around ±10 days) without established longer-horizon drift estimates.

### Cited Findings
- Womack (1996) found a mean abnormal return of 2.4% in the one month following analyst upgrades, while downgrade abnormal returns were statistically indistinguishable from zero over the same one-month window — [Talval: Do Analyst Upgrades Beat the Market?](https://talval.com/learn/do-analyst-upgrades-beat-the-market)
- Post-recommendation-change price drift is "stronger after downgrades, stronger for changes accompanied by earnings-estimate revisions in the same direction, stronger for higher-reputation analysts, and decaying with time, front-loaded in the first several weeks" — [Talval: Do Analyst Upgrades Beat the Market?](https://talval.com/learn/do-analyst-upgrades-beat-the-market)
- Practitioner characterization: price drift after an analyst rating change "often continues for one to four weeks after the initial pop," with institutional buyers scaling in and retail chasing the headline; after that window "momentum typically flattens or reverses" — [Talval: Do Analyst Upgrades Beat the Market?](https://talval.com/learn/do-analyst-upgrades-beat-the-market)
- At the three-month mark, one study found upgrades land at exactly 50.0% (median excess return of zero), suggesting the recommendation-drift effect has largely dissipated by that horizon — [Talval: Do Analyst Upgrades Beat the Market?](https://talval.com/learn/do-analyst-upgrades-beat-the-market)
- Buyback (share repurchase) announcements: strong evidence of positive long-run abnormal drift for 3-4 years after both tender-offer and open-market repurchase announcements, historically interpreted as market underreaction to the buyback signal — [ScienceDirect: Long-run stock performance following stock repurchases](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1062976910000268)
- For underpriced firms specifically, long-term abnormal performance after repurchase announcements is positive and significant, in the range of 11%-25% over the 12-36 month period post-announcement — [ECGI working paper: Are Buybacks Good for Long-Term Shareholder Value?](https://www.ecgi.global/sites/default/files/working_papers/documents/finalmanconipeyervermaelen_0.pdf)
- Buyback drift magnitude has weakened over time: long-horizon abnormal returns following repurchase announcements made after 2001 are much lower than for pre-1994 announcements, i.e., the anomaly has partially been arbitraged away in more recent decades — [ScienceDirect: Repurchases after being well known as good news](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0929119919309368)
- M&A / merger-arbitrage: deal (and hence spread-convergence) timelines range "from as quickly as 45 days under a tender offer to multiple years under a merger with complex regulatory risk" — [Verdad: Merger Arbitrage](https://verdadcap.com/archive/merger-arbitrage)
- In a smoothly-proceeding deal, the arbitrage spread "grinds steadily toward zero as the closing date approaches," with convergence driven by discrete milestone events (antitrust clearance, shareholder vote, financing lock) rather than a smooth time-decay process — [Verdad: Merger Arbitrage](https://verdadcap.com/archive/merger-arbitrage)
- Example given for the spread/time relationship: a 10% spread deal closing in 6 months and a 5% spread deal closing in 3 months both annualize to a 20% return, illustrating that spread size scales with expected time-to-close in this literature — [Verdad: Merger Arbitrage](https://verdadcap.com/archive/merger-arbitrage)
- Contract/order-win announcement event studies (e.g., a pharmaceutical-industry study of 327 event days across 12 NASDAQ-listed pharma companies, 2010-2021) typically use a ±10 trading day event window around the announcement and find a significant positive cumulative abnormal return reaction, though daily average abnormal returns within the window are not always individually significant — [Anser Press: Effect of Public Contract Winning Announcements on Share Prices](https://www.anserpress.org/journal/jea/3/1/43)
- Winning contractors show a positive stock reaction attributable either to a "winner's curse" argument or a "cost revelation" argument, per the same study — [Anser Press: Effect of Public Contract Winning Announcements on Share Prices](https://www.anserpress.org/journal/jea/3/1/43)

### Inferences
- There is a rough event-type hierarchy of expected drift duration: analyst-recommendation drift (shortest, ~1-4 weeks) < earnings/PEAD (~60-90 trading days, i.e., about a quarter) < buyback-announcement drift (longest, 12-48 months). M&A "drift" is qualitatively different — it is not a diffusing/decaying informational drift but a spread that mechanically converges to zero at a scheduled/uncertain closing date, so it is better modeled with a deal-timeline/hazard-based estimate than a decay curve.
- Contract/order-win announcements appear to be the least-studied event type for post-event drift duration; existing academic event studies focus almost exclusively on the immediate ±10-day reaction and do not report longer-horizon (multi-week to multi-month) persistence estimates. This is a genuine literature gap, not just a search-coverage gap.
- For a systematic Event Watcher, this suggests using event-type-specific duration priors (e.g., ~2-3 weeks for analyst actions, ~1 quarter for earnings surprises, ~several quarters/years for buybacks, and a deal-completion-hazard model rather than a fixed duration for M&A) rather than one universal duration constant.

### Gaps
- No source found provides a quantitative "half-life" or day-count specifically for contract/order-win announcement drift beyond the initial ±10-day event window; longer-horizon persistence for this event type is unclear and may need to be estimated empirically from the Event Watcher's own historical data rather than from published literature.
- No direct numeric comparison found between contract-announcement drift duration and earnings-drift duration; this comparison (explicitly requested in the assignment) could not be sourced and should be flagged as an open question, or answered from proprietary in-house backtests instead of public literature.

## How do quant researchers empirically define and estimate "duration" of a move (CAR peak timing, reversion thresholds, exponential half-life, survival analysis, autocorrelation decay)?

### Takeaway
No single dominant formal-duration methodology emerged from the search; the literature more often reports either (a) fixed conventional windows (e.g., 60/90/180 trading days) without fitting an explicit decay curve, or (b) autocorrelation-of-returns statistics (as in Bernard & Thomas 1990) that could be repurposed into a half-life estimate. Survival-analysis (Kaplan-Meier) methodology is well established in general finance for duration-to-event problems (e.g., time-to-default, time-to-crisis-end) and has been applied to price-decline "duration dependence," but no source directly applied Kaplan-Meier/hazard modeling to post-news drift/CAR duration specifically — this appears to be a methodological opportunity rather than settled practice.

### Cited Findings
- Standard event-study methodology defines cumulative abnormal return (CAR) as the sum of daily abnormal returns (actual return minus a modeled "normal" return, typically from a market or factor model estimated over a pre-event clean period) accumulated across the days in the event window — [Princeton event study guide](https://libguides.princeton.edu/eventstudy); [Oscar Torres-Reyna, Estimation of Abnormal Returns](https://www.princeton.edu/~otorres/AbnormalReturns.pdf)
- Over long horizons, CAR/BHAR estimates are noted to depend heavily on the choice of benchmark model, small errors in expected returns compound over time, and the statistical distribution of long-run abnormal returns is "badly behaved" — a caution about fitting precise decay/half-life curves to long-horizon CAR series — [ScienceDirect: Tests for cumulative abnormal returns over long periods: Simulation evidence](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/1057521993900064)
- Survival analysis (time-to-event modeling, e.g., Kaplan-Meier) is described generally as well suited to financial duration questions because it handles censoring — cases where the "event" (e.g., full reversion, or drift ending) has not yet been observed by the end of the sample — [bookdown: A short course on Survival Analysis applied to the Financial Industry](https://bookdown.org/sestelo/sa_financial/introduction.html)
- A study of financial-crisis/price-decline duration dependence in the S&P 500 (1871-2016) applied Kaplan-Meier survival curves to price declines, finding survival probabilities starting around 0.50 in non-recession periods versus 0.62 in recession periods — demonstrating the technique's applicability to price-move duration questions generally (though this specific study is about crisis/decline duration, not single-news-event drift) — [arXiv: Hysteresis and Duration Dependence of Financial Crises in the US](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.00259)
- Bernard & Thomas (1990)'s autocorrelation-of-quarterly-SUE-related-returns figures (lag 1 ≈ 0.34, lag 2 ≈ 0.19, lag 3 ≈ 0.06, lag 4 ≈ -0.24) show an approximately geometric decay pattern in the first three lags before reversing sign, which is structurally similar to what a half-life/AR(1) decay fit would produce — [Wikipedia: PEAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift)

### Inferences
- The "days until CAR peaks" and "days until reversion to X% of peak" definitions mentioned in the assignment's key questions appear to be practitioner/systematic-trading framings rather than standard academic terminology — academic event studies mostly report drift over fixed windows (e.g., 60, 90, 180, 250 days) rather than fitting an explicit peak-and-decay curve. A systematic Event Watcher module wanting a genuine half-life estimate would likely need to compute it empirically in-house (e.g., fit an exponential or AR(1) decay to its own historical CAR curves per event type) rather than import a single number from the literature, since the literature does not converge on one.
- Survival analysis / hazard modeling is a methodologically sound but underused approach for this specific problem (duration of a post-news drift) — the closest precedents found are in unrelated domains (crisis duration, customer churn, deal completion) rather than event-drift literature directly, suggesting this would be a novel application worth flagging as a build-it-yourself methodology rather than one with off-the-shelf published parameters.

### Gaps
- No source found gives a directly-computed "half-life of CAR decay" number for any event type (earnings, M&A, contract wins, etc.) — this number likely does not exist in a readily citable public form and would need to be derived from raw data.
- Could not find any published application of Kaplan-Meier/hazard survival methodology specifically to "how long does a post-earnings (or other event) abnormal-return drift last" — only adjacent applications (crisis duration, corporate survival, virtual-goods lifetime) were found.

## Is there literature/practitioner methodology on volatility decay after a news shock (e.g., GARCH-type volatility half-life) that could serve as a "digestion time" proxy?

### Takeaway
GARCH-model volatility half-life is a well-defined, formulaic, and empirically estimated concept — half-life = ln(0.5) / ln(α+β), where α (shock/ARCH term) governs sensitivity to new information and β (GARCH term) governs persistence — and real-world instrument-level estimates (e.g., ~42 trading days for S&P 500, ~38 days for US Treasuries, ~16 days for oil) show it is asset-class dependent, offering a plausible, computable proxy for "how long the market keeps digesting" a shock.

### Cited Findings
- Formula: half-life of a GARCH volatility shock ≈ ln(0.5) / ln(α + β), where α measures how strongly new information (a large return) affects variance and β measures how much of the prior period's conditional variance persists — [Ryan O'Connell CFA: EWMA and GARCH Models Explained](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/volatility-estimation-garch/)
- The sum (α + β), called "persistence," determines how slowly volatility decays back to its long-run average level after a shock; a higher β means volatility decays more slowly following a shock — [Ryan O'Connell CFA: EWMA and GARCH Models Explained](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/volatility-estimation-garch/)
- Empirical half-life examples from NYU Stern V-Lab-style GARCH analyses: S&P 500 Index volatility shocks decay with an estimated half-life of about 42 trading days; US Treasuries take roughly 38 days to reach half-life (reflecting higher persistence); oil shows a shorter half-life of roughly 16 days — [search-result synthesis referencing NYU V-Lab GARCH pages](https://vlab.stern.nyu.edu/volatility/VOL.SPX:IND-R.GARCH)
- For most financial assets, α + β is typically close to 1 (i.e., near-integrated variance), implying volatility shocks generally decay slowly rather than quickly — [Ryan O'Connell CFA: EWMA and GARCH Models Explained](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/volatility-estimation-garch/)

### Inferences
- A GARCH-based volatility half-life, computed per-instrument (or per-sector) from a stock's own return series, is a readily implementable, purely price-based proxy an Event Watcher module could use to bound the expected "digestion window" for any news shock on that name — e.g., if a stock's own GARCH(1,1) volatility half-life is ~30-40 trading days, that is a reasonable prior upper bound for how long an event-driven swing might remain elevated/relevant, independent of the event's fundamental type.
- Because the half-life is instrument-specific (equity index ~42 days vs. oil ~16 days in the cited examples), a single global constant is not appropriate; the module should estimate α, β per underlying (or per liquidity/sector bucket) rather than hard-coding one duration number.
- Note the important conceptual distinction: GARCH volatility half-life measures decay of *return variance* (how long a stock stays "jumpy" after a shock), not decay of *directional drift* (how long the price keeps trending in the surprise's direction) — the two are related digestion-time proxies but are not the same quantity, and the research question conflated them somewhat; the report should treat GARCH half-life as a volatility-side cross-check, not a direct drift-duration estimate.

### Gaps
- Could not verify the specific per-instrument half-life numbers (42 days S&P 500, 38 days Treasuries, 16 days oil) against the original NYU V-Lab source pages directly (only reached via secondary search synthesis) — these should be treated as indicative/illustrative rather than rigorously confirmed, and ideally re-derived from the Event Watcher's own return data using the standard GARCH(1,1) half-life formula.
- No source found specifically studies GARCH volatility half-life estimated in an event-conditional way (i.e., the shock parameter re-estimated specifically around scheduled corporate news events, versus general unconditional daily-return GARCH fitting) — general GARCH literature (Engle et al.) covers unconditional volatility clustering, not an event-triggered variant.

## Any known typical numeric ranges across event types, and how do options markets imply expected move duration/decay as a cross-check?

### Takeaway
Numeric ranges gathered across event types: momentum (3-12 month formation/holding, reversing at 12-18 months) is the longest-duration systematic effect studied; PEAD (~60-90 trading days) and analyst-recommendation drift (~1-4 weeks) are shorter; GARCH volatility half-lives (~16-42 trading days depending on instrument) sit in between. Options markets encode a related but distinct concept — an inverted/humped implied-volatility term structure around a known event date reflects the market pricing a short, sharp event-day volatility component that is expected to collapse quickly afterward, providing a market-implied (rather than historical-statistical) cross-check on expected move duration.

### Cited Findings
- Jegadeesh & Titman-style momentum: stocks that perform best (worst) over a 3-12 month formation period tend to continue outperforming (underperforming) over the subsequent 3-12 month holding period — the "momentum effect" — [Foxholm Financial: Jegadeesh & Titman Momentum](https://foxholm.com/q/research/jegadeesh-titman-momentum/); [Blank Capital Research: Jegadeesh & Titman](https://blankcapitalresearch.com/learn/jegadeesh-titman-momentum)
- Momentum profits are documented to partially reverse after 12-18 months — [search synthesis of momentum literature](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378426614003252)
- No source located gives an explicit "half-life in months" figure attributed directly to Jegadeesh & Titman (1993) itself; the paper's own framing is in terms of formation/holding-period returns rather than a fitted decay half-life — this specific practitioner-style statistic could not be confirmed as originating from the original paper.
- Options/volatility term structure: implied volatility term structure describes how implied vol varies by expiration, normally upward-sloping (contango) in calm markets — [QuestDB glossary: Implied Volatility Term Structure](https://questdb.com/glossary/implied-volatility-term-structure/)
- Around an anticipated event (e.g., earnings, drug-trial readout), the term structure often becomes inverted/humped because near-term option expirations must price in an extra "event-day" volatility component; traders decompose implied volatility into the event-day-specific component and the "typical" daily volatility to back out a market-implied expected move size for the event day itself — [Moontower Substack: how an option trader extracts earnings from a vol term structure](https://moontower.substack.com/p/how-an-option-trader-extracts-earnings)
- Because implied volatility is strongly mean-reverting, a near-term shock is expected to decay over time such that longer-dated implied volatilities move by only a fractional amount in response — though empirically, a rise in short-dated IV is often accompanied by a disproportionately large rise in long-dated IV too, showing near-term uncertainty shocks do bleed into longer-horizon expectations more than a pure "one-day-only" event model would predict — [IBKR Quant Campus: Mastering Options Volatility Term Structure](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/campus/ibkr-quant-news/mastering-options-volatility-term-structure/)
- An inverted term structure ahead of a known event (like an earnings release) is described as common precisely because the event is "expected to cause short-term volatility but is unlikely to have a lasting impact on the market" — i.e., the market's own default assumption embedded in options pricing is that most single corporate news events are short-duration, not persistent drift-inducing events — [IBKR Quant Campus](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/campus/ibkr-quant-news/mastering-options-volatility-term-structure/)

### Inferences
- There is a meaningful tension worth flagging to the report writer: the options market's own pricing convention (term-structure inversion collapsing quickly post-event) implicitly assumes most event-driven volatility is short-lived (essentially one to a few days), which is in some tension with the equity literature's finding of drift persisting for weeks to months (PEAD, buybacks) — the options market appears to price the *volatility* shock as short-duration even when the *directional drift* empirically persists much longer. This suggests options-implied duration and empirically-realized drift duration are answering different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable duration estimates.
- Ranking by typical duration found across all research questions (approximate, from shortest to longest): contract/order-win reaction window as directly studied (~10 days, but longer-horizon persistence unstudied) < analyst-recommendation drift (1-4 weeks, mostly gone by 3 months) < GARCH-volatility half-life (~16-42 trading days, instrument-dependent) < PEAD/earnings drift (~60-90 trading days, about one quarter, with re-acceleration around next earnings) < momentum (3-12 month formation and holding, partial reversal by 12-18 months) < buyback-announcement drift (12-48 months). This ordering is a reasonable prior structure for an Event Watcher's default duration-by-event-type table, pending in-house empirical validation.

### Gaps
- No numeric, citable "momentum half-life in months" statistic could be found attributed to Jegadeesh & Titman (1993) or a clearly-identified later replication; only qualitative formation/holding-period windows (3-12 months) and a reversal onset (12-18 months) were found. If a report needs a specific half-life number for momentum, it was not located in this pass and should be flagged as unconfirmed/unavailable rather than estimated.
- No source found that directly computes an "implied expected-move duration" number (e.g., in days) derived from IV term-structure changes around a specific event type (earnings vs. M&A vs. contract win) — the mechanism (event-day vol extraction from term structure) is described qualitatively but no specific day-count or half-life output value from this method was located.
