# Detecting and Exiting Failed Horizontal Breakouts Quickly (Early-Stop Side)

## What price/volume patterns define a "failed" or "false" breakout?

### Takeaway
The consensus definition across both classical technical-analysis research (Bulkowski) and O'Neil/IBD-style systematic methodology is: a breakout "fails" when price closes back inside the prior base/range (below the breakout pivot / prior resistance), as opposed to a "throwback" or "pullback" (a normal retest of the breakout level that does NOT re-enter the range and does not invalidate the pattern). A fixed percentage decline below the pivot (7–8% in the O'Neil system) is the most commonly cited quantitative failure trigger computable from daily closes alone.

### Cited Findings
- Bulkowski defines a breakout as failing ("break-even failure") when it does not move at least 5% in the breakout direction before reversing; for rectangle bottoms the break-even failure rate is 15% (upward breakouts) and 24% (downward breakouts); for rectangle tops it is 15% (upward) and 34% (downward), based on 900+ and 1,000+ "perfect trades" respectively — [Bulkowski Rectangle Bottoms](https://thepatternsite.com/rectbots.html), [Bulkowski Rectangle Tops](https://thepatternsite.com/recttops.html)
- Bulkowski's broader failure-rate study (23 pattern types, 1991–2008): failure to climb at least 10% averaged 14% in the 1990s bull market and rose to 28% in 2003–2007; at a 20% threshold, failure rates rose from 22% (1991) to 64% (2007); at 40%, from 64% to 88%. For downward breakouts, the 10% failure rate averaged 26% in the 1990s and rose to 49% in 2003–2007 — [Bulkowski's Failure Rate Study](https://thepatternsite.com/FailureRates.html)
- Throwbacks/pullbacks (price returning to retest the breakout level without closing back inside the range) occur in roughly 53–66% of all breakouts across pattern types and are explicitly described as normal, NOT indicative of failure: for rectangle bottoms throwback/pullback rate is 64%/66%; for rectangle tops 66%/64%; across 10,000+ patterns generally, throwbacks occur ~53% of the time and pullbacks ~56% — [Bulkowski Rectangle Bottoms](https://thepatternsite.com/rectbots.html), [Bulkowski Rectangle Tops](https://thepatternsite.com/recttops.html)
- If a throwback/pullback is going to happen, it typically completes within 3 to 10 days of the breakout — [Bulkowski on Pullbacks](https://www.thepatternsite.com/pullbacks.html)
- Practitioner/quant framing: "If price falls back and closes inside the old range (below the breakout level), consider exiting — breakout failed. Similarly, if price re-enters the range and closes inside it, exit early, invalid breakout." — [LuxAlgo: 5 False Breakout Strategies](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/5-false-breakout-strategies-for-traders/)
- A systematic breakout-scanner discussion (Qullamaggie-style opening-range/momentum breakout) proposed defining failure as: "a close above resistance followed within three bars by a close back below it," with entry at the next open after confirmation — [Qullamaggie Breakout Scanner Issue #7](https://github.com/VladPetrariu/Qullamaggie-breakout-scanner/issues/7)
- A study on O'Neil-style breakouts found: "71% of stock breakouts are currently back below their pivot point, with about 43% having clearly failed and being at least -10% or more below the pivot," and "without adequate breakout volume, the failure rate roughly doubles compared to volume-confirmed breakouts" (numbers from a secondary summary; primary O'Neil Global Advisors PDF titled "Breakouts: Pump Up the Volume" could not be extracted as text — it appears to be an image/vector-based PDF — so this stat should be treated as lower-confidence pending primary-source confirmation) — [O'Neil Global Advisors PDF (unreadable as text)](https://www.oneilglobaladvisors.com/documents/FG/oneil/research/617948_OGA_Breakouts-OGA.pdf), summarized via [Forbes: How To Use Breakdowns In The O'Neil Methodology](https://www.forbes.com/sites/randywatts/2023/03/29/how-to-use-breakdowns-in-the-oneil-methodology/)

### Inferences
- A computable, day-close-only failure rule consistent with this literature: exit if the daily close falls back below the breakout pivot AND stays there (i.e., closes below it) — optionally requiring the close to be a fixed % below pivot (5% per Bulkowski's break-even definition, or 7-8% per O'Neil) rather than reacting to any single wick/close below the exact pivot price, to avoid whipsawing on normal noise.
- Because throwbacks/pullbacks to the breakout level are extremely common (>50% of all breakouts) and usually resolve within 3-10 days, an exit rule triggered merely by "price touches or dips slightly below the pivot intraday" would generate excessive false exits; the rule should require a CLOSE below the level (not intraday touch) and ideally look for closing back inside the base/range, not just at the pivot line.

### Gaps
- No source gave a precise, single canonical percentage threshold (e.g., "closes 3% below pivot = failure") specifically for horizontal-base breakouts distinct from Bulkowski's general 5%/10% break-even-failure convention or O'Neil's 7-8% rule; these are the two most citable anchor numbers found.
- The O'Neil Global Advisors "Breakouts: Pump Up the Volume" primary study could not be read as text (binary/vector PDF); only secondary-source paraphrases were available, so those specific percentages (71% back below pivot, 43% down 10%+) carry lower confidence and are not independently verified against the original document.

## What time-based failure rules ("time stops") are used, and what X%/Y-day values are typical?

### Takeaway
Time stops in this space are less standardized numerically than price-based stops, but several concrete figures recur: exiting/reassessing if a breakout doesn't show meaningful follow-through within roughly 3-10 days (matching the throwback/pullback completion window), and separately, momentum-breakout research showing ~26% of breakouts are still down 21 days later. No single authoritative "X% in Y days" rule dominates the literature; most X/Y combinations found are anecdotal or from smaller/independent backtests rather than large published studies.

### Cited Findings
- Bulkowski: throwbacks/pullbacks, when they occur, typically complete within 3 to 10 days of the breakout — this is effectively the window during which a genuine early failure should reveal itself via a close back below the pivot — [Bulkowski on Pullbacks](https://www.thepatternsite.com/pullbacks.html)
- A quant momentum-breakout backtest found "about 26% of filtered breakouts are down 21 days later, averaging -3.5%" — giving one concrete data point for a ~1-month time-stop failure rate, though the filtering methodology (pattern type, market, timeframe) was not specified in the summary retrieved — [Medium: The Momentum Breakout Strategy (FMZQuant)](https://medium.com/@FMZQuant/the-momentum-breakout-strategy-fb2c3c760c8e)
- An opening-range-breakout confirmation study (77 fills) found that closing above vs. below the entry/fill price on the SAME DAY was highly predictive: closes above entry averaged +0.15R (55% win rate) vs. closes below entry averaging -0.16R (41% win rate); notably 51% of live fills closed beneath entry on the fill day itself, and volume was often inadequate (only 17% reached 1.5x average volume) — suggesting same-day (Y=0-1 day) confirmation has real discriminating power even before a formal X% threshold is applied — [Qullamaggie Breakout Scanner Issue #7](https://github.com/VladPetrariu/Qullamaggie-breakout-scanner/issues/7)
- General swing-trading commentary notes "90% of stocks give most of their movement in just 3-4 days" after a breakout, used informally to justify short time-stop windows, though this is presented anecdotally without a cited study — [Trade That Swing: Breakout and Run Strategy](https://tradethatswing.com/the-breakout-and-run-day-trading-strategy/)

### Inferences
- Combining the above, a reasonable computable time-stop for horizontal breakout failure in the 1-90 day holding range would check follow-through at two checkpoints: (1) T+1 to T+3 days (same-day/next-few-day confirmation, per the opening-range-breakout data), and (2) T+10 days (matching Bulkowski's throwback/pullback completion window) — if the stock has not closed above the pivot with some minimum gain (or has closed back below it) by ~day 10, the setup is statistically more likely to be a failure than a "resting" winner.
- The 21-day, -3.5%-average, 26%-failure-rate figure from the momentum backtest could serve as a longer secondary checkpoint, but its source methodology is not fully documented and should be flagged as a single independent (non-peer-reviewed) backtest rather than an industry-standard figure.

### Gaps
- No large, well-documented, named published study (e.g., from O'Neil Global Advisors, IBD, or an academic paper) was found that specifies an exact "X% gain within Y trading days, else exit" time-stop rule for horizontal/base breakouts. The X/Y values found (3-10 days for throwback resolution, 21 days/-3.5%/26% for momentum breakouts, "3-4 days" for most movement) come from a mix of a respected pattern-statistics source (Bulkowski, for the day-count) and independent/informal backtests (for the % figures), not from a single authoritative source combining both dimensions.
- Could not verify whether O'Neil/IBD literature has a formal time-stop rule (they were only found to have the 7-8% price-based stop, not an explicit day-count-based time stop) — this should be treated as a gap rather than assumed absent.

## What is the statistical base rate of breakout failure (published studies, cite numbers)?

### Takeaway
Multiple independent sources converge on failure rates in the ~15-30% range for a moderate (~10%) failure threshold in favorable market conditions, rising sharply (to 50-88%) for larger move thresholds or in less favorable (already-extended) bull-market periods; volume confirmation roughly halves the failure rate.

### Cited Findings
- Bulkowski (23 pattern types, 1991-2008): failure to reach 10% averaged 14% in the 1990s bull market, rising to 28% in 2003-2007; at 20%, failure rose from 22% to 64%; at 40%, from 64% to 88% (upward breakouts). Downward breakout 10% failure rate: 26% (1990s) to 49% (2003-2007) — [Bulkowski's Failure Rate Study](https://thepatternsite.com/FailureRates.html)
- Rectangle-pattern-specific break-even failure rates (a stricter definition — failing to move even 5%): 15% (rectangle bottoms/tops, upward breakout), 24% (rectangle bottoms, downward breakout), 34% (rectangle tops, downward breakout) — [Bulkowski Rectangle Bottoms](https://thepatternsite.com/rectbots.html), [Bulkowski Rectangle Tops](https://thepatternsite.com/recttops.html)
- A secondary-source summary of O'Neil-style breakout research states 71% of breakouts are at some point back below their pivot point, ~43% clearly failed (10%+ below pivot); failure rate roughly doubles without adequate breakout volume (flagged lower-confidence — primary PDF unreadable) — [Forbes summary of O'Neil methodology](https://www.forbes.com/sites/randywatts/2023/03/29/how-to-use-breakdowns-in-the-oneil-methodology/)
- IBD's 7-8% sell-rule context: in one cited study, "15% of 39 breakouts triggered the 7% to 8% sell rule and 25% underperformed the Nasdaq" when following IBD's full buying criteria (proper base, volume, fundamentals) — a small sample (n=39) but a directly-cited figure — [ChartMill: William O'Neil's 7-8% Sell Rule Explained](https://www.chartmill.com/documentation/trading-and-investing/methodologies/527-William-ONeils-7-8-Sell-Rule-Explained)
- Momentum-breakout backtest: ~26% of filtered breakouts down 21 days later, averaging -3.5% — [Medium: FMZQuant Momentum Breakout Strategy](https://medium.com/@FMZQuant/the-momentum-breakout-strategy-fb2c3c760c8e)
- IBD notes later-stage bases (3rd stage and beyond) fail more often "because the story is widely known by then" (qualitative, no % given) — [search summary citing IBD methodology, via O'Neil/Forbes sources]

### Inferences
- The wide range of quoted failure rates (14% to 71%, depending on threshold, definition of "failure," and market regime) means any single number should always be reported alongside its definition (threshold %, market period, pattern type) — there is no single universal "breakout failure rate."
- Volume confirmation is repeatedly cited (Bulkowski's implicit volume studies, and the O'Neil-derived claim) as roughly halving failure rates, making volume-on-breakout a first-order filter before any time/price failure rule is even applied.

### Gaps
- Could not directly read/verify the O'Neil Global Advisors "Breakouts: Pump Up the Volume" primary PDF (fetched but returned as unreadable binary/vector content) — its statistics are reported here only via a secondary Forbes summary and should be treated as lower-confidence until the primary source is confirmed by a human reader with proper PDF tools.
- The IBD n=39 sample study is very small; no larger-sample IBD/O'Neil study with a clean failure-rate percentage (specifically for the 7-8% stop being triggered) was found.

## How do practitioners distinguish an ordinary pullback/shakeout from a genuine failure?

### Takeaway
The primary, well-documented distinguishing factor is whether price closes back INSIDE the prior base/range (failure) versus merely retesting the breakout line from above/below without closing back inside it (throwback/pullback, normal). Bulkowski's data shows the majority of breakouts experience some retest, so the key computable distinguishing signal is close location relative to the range, not mere proximity to the pivot.

### Cited Findings
- Definitional distinction: "A pullback is when price temporarily moves against the underlying trend... When price breaks out of a trading range but fails to retain its momentum and later re-enters the trading range, it's called a false breakout" — [Tradingpedia: Breakout Pullbacks](https://www.tradingpedia.com/price-action-trading/breakout-pullbacks/)
- Throwbacks/pullbacks are described by Bulkowski as "a normal part of price discovery" and explicitly "shouldn't be confused with failed breakouts" — occurring in the majority of cases (53-66% depending on pattern) without invalidating the breakout — [Bulkowski Rectangle Bottoms/Tops](https://thepatternsite.com/rectbots.html)
- Practical rule from a systematic-trading write-up: exit "if price re-enters the range and closes inside it" (i.e., a close-based re-entry into the base, not an intraday wick) — [LuxAlgo: 5 False Breakout Strategies](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/5-false-breakout-strategies-for-traders/)
- Shakeouts (a brief, sharp move beyond the range meant to trigger stops before reversing back in the intended breakout direction) are described as typically moving 10-15%+ in a very short period before reversing — though this figure is presented without a rigorous study citation and appears to be a general/anecdotal guideline rather than a backtested threshold — [search-summarized guideline, unverified primary source]
- O'Neil/IBD's own answer to this question is effectively the 7-8% rule itself: a decline of that magnitude below the buy point (not day-count based) is treated as the dividing line between an acceptable pullback and an unacceptable failure, deliberately set "tight enough to stop large losses, but wide enough to avoid being stopped out by normal day-to-day noise around a breakout" — [ChartMill: William O'Neil's 7-8% Sell Rule Explained](https://www.chartmill.com/documentation/trading-and-investing/methodologies/527-William-ONeils-7-8-Sell-Rule-Explained)

### Inferences
- The two dominant, independently-sourced heuristics for the pullback-vs-failure boundary are: (a) close-based re-entry into the prior range/base (structural definition, from TA literature), and (b) a fixed 7-8% decline from the pivot/buy point (magnitude-based definition, from O'Neil/IBD). A computable combined rule could require BOTH — a close back inside the base AND/OR a close 7-8% below the pivot — to reduce false positives from either measure alone.
- Volume on the reversal day is repeatedly implied (though not given a specific numeric threshold in the sources found) as a secondary confirming factor: a reversal on volume above the recent average is treated as more meaningful than a low-volume dip.

### Gaps
- No source gave a precise, backtested volume threshold (e.g., "reversal volume > 1.5x average signals real failure vs. < 1.0x average is noise") specific to the failure/pullback distinction — this remains qualitative ("volume on the reversal day matters") rather than quantified in the sources found.
- The 10-15%+ shakeout magnitude figure is unsourced/anecdotal (traced to a blog-style summary, not a primary study) and should be flagged as low-confidence.

## Are there quantitative studies on "close back below breakout level" as an exit trigger, and how many confirmation days are typically required?

### Takeaway
Evidence points toward same-day or very-short (1-3 bar) confirmation being both the most commonly proposed AND empirically supported approach in the sources found — waiting for extended (multi-week) confirmation defeats the purpose of an early-stop rule — but only one small, non-academic backtest (n=77) with actual same-day statistics was located; no large published academic or O'Neil/IBD study isolating "close back below breakout level" alone (independent of the 7-8% magnitude rule) was found.

### Cited Findings
- A concrete same-day-confirmation backtest (n=77 opening-range breakout fills): closing above the entry/fill price same-day → +0.15R avg, 55% win rate; closing below → -0.16R avg, 41% win rate; the proposed exit rule was "if the fill-day close is below the fill price, exit at that close," expected to cap losses near -0.2R vs. a typical -1R for breakouts left unmanaged — [Qullamaggie Breakout Scanner Issue #7](https://github.com/VladPetrariu/Qullamaggie-breakout-scanner/issues/7)
- An alternative, slightly more conservative confirmation window proposed in the same discussion: "a close above resistance followed within three bars by a close back below it" as the failure definition, with entry only after a confirmed close above the level (a 2-step entry/exit framework, using a 3-bar/3-day lookback rather than same-day) — [Qullamaggie Breakout Scanner Issue #7](https://github.com/VladPetrariu/Qullamaggie-breakout-scanner/issues/7)
- Bulkowski's data implies a natural outer bound for confirmation: since throwbacks/pullbacks (which should NOT trigger an exit) typically resolve within 3-10 days, a "close back below the level" exit rule should ideally fire within that same window if it's going to be a genuine early-failure signal, rather than waiting weeks — [Bulkowski on Pullbacks](https://www.thepatternsite.com/pullbacks.html)
- O'Neil/IBD's 7-8% rule is magnitude-based, not day-count/confirmation-based — it is typically applied as soon as the loss threshold is crossed, i.e., effectively same-day/no confirmation delay, "sold without debate" — [ChartMill: William O'Neil's 7-8% Sell Rule Explained](https://www.chartmill.com/documentation/trading-and-investing/methodologies/527-William-ONeils-7-8-Sell-Rule-Explained)

### Inferences
- Across the sources found, confirmation windows for a "close back below breakout level" rule cluster at the short end: same-day (0 days) up to about 3 days/bars. No source proposed multi-week confirmation for an early-failure exit — this is expected, since the entire purpose of this exit type (per the task's framing) is to cut losses fast, before a wider trailing stop would trigger.
- Given the small sample size (n=77) of the only quantified same-day study found, and the qualitative/anecdotal nature of the 3-bar alternative, this is an area where the published/backtested evidence base is thin relative to the price-magnitude-based rules (Bulkowski %, O'Neil 7-8%), which rest on much larger samples (900-10,000+ patterns).

### Gaps
- No large-sample, published, or peer-reviewed study was found that isolates "close back below breakout level" (independent of a percentage-magnitude threshold) as a standalone exit trigger and reports its win rate / failure rate across a sizeable universe of breakouts. The only quantified same-day-confirmation data point (n=77) is from an independent GitHub discussion/backtest of a specific scanner project, not a vetted academic or industry publication, and should be weighted accordingly.
- Could not find a direct comparison study of "same-day close" vs. "2-3 day confirmation" vs. "no confirmation (intraday trigger)" run on the same dataset to quantify the tradeoff between avoiding whipsaws (from waiting for confirmation) and cutting losses faster (from acting same-day) — this would be valuable but was not located within the search budget for this assignment.
